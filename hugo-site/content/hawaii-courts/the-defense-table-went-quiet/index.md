The State built its record through witness testimony, numbered exhibits, and arguments in court. Defense counsel had to respond through cross-examination, objections, offers of proof, motions, testimony, consultation, and preservation of issues for review.

Privately retained counsel represented me in a felony jury trial. I paid the additional trial fee because I wanted the case tried and repeatedly rejected a plea.

After Kanemoto’s mock-pistol closing, the judge immediately called counsel into chambers. No mistrial motion was made in my presence. While the same jury continued deciding the felony case, counsel pressed a petty-misdemeanor plea. I refused and wanted the jury to finish. He kept pressing.

The jury hung. Counsel told me there would be another trial, then called back about a day later sounding pleased and said the prosecutor would not retry. I later obtained an expungement.

His file could show what happened in chambers, what remedies he considered, when the plea became available, and what he explained to me.

> **Editorial anonymization:** The lawyer remains identified only as **Retained Trial Counsel**. The investigation concerns conduct, incentives, records, and professional duties.

## I. The Client Owned the Objective

In a criminal case, counsel controls many tactical choices. The client decides whether to plead and, after consultation, whether to testify. Hawaiʻi Rule of Professional Conduct 1.2(a), in force during the 2017 trial period, assigns those decisions to the client.[^1] *Tachibana v. State* recognizes the decision whether to take the stand as the defendant’s personal constitutional right.[^10] That right does not make every subject the defendant wants to discuss admissible.

I wanted a jury trial. I repeatedly rejected a plea, told counsel to stop pressing it, and testified at trial. I wanted the jury to hear about the earlier pressure that explained my state of mind during the November 2015 tax-office encounter. The pressure that came afterward affected my defense preparation and willingness to disclose what had happened.

Counsel had to advise me, test the State's case, identify available remedies, explain material developments, and exercise independent judgment in my interest.

I want the records to answer six questions.

1. What did counsel tell the jury in opening?
2. What did he do with the state's principal witness?
3. What advice did he give me about the content of my testimony, and how was my decision to omit a subject recorded?
4. What remedies did he consider after the mock-pistol closing?
5. What information did he give me before pressing the petty-misdemeanor plea?
6. Did counsel's own financial or personal interests foreclose a course that reasonably should have been pursued for the client?

The sixth question comes directly from Hawaiʻi conflict doctrine.

## II. The Fee Structure and the “Tight Trial”

My retainer had two tiers: **&#36;6,000** for plea representation and **&#36;8,000** for a full jury trial. Plea representation accounted for 75 percent of the maximum stated fee. The additional fee for taking the felony through a jury trial was $2,000.

A more favorable plea position already existed when I paid the extra $2,000 and chose trial.

Before trial, counsel told me, in substance, that **the judge preferred a “tight trial.”** I heard that before the decisions about my testimony, the response to the mock-pistol closing, and the plea pressure during deliberations. The remark does not tell me why he made those choices.

The fixed-fee agreement said nothing about retrials. What would a second trial require of counsel, what additional fee could he collect, and could he withdraw? Those questions mattered once the closing raised the possibility of a mistrial.

Comment [5] to Hawaiʻi Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5, in force in 2017, warns against fee terms that could lead a lawyer to cut services improperly or act against the client's interests. It also addresses the need to explain foreseeable additional work to the client.[^17] Whether the fee was reasonable and whether its terms affected the defense are separate questions. The retainer and counsel's file could show whether the prospect of unpaid retrial work affected his preparation, choice of remedies, advice, or continued pressure to plead.

The retainer and engagement correspondence could establish whether a second trial would bring new compensation or unpaid work:

- **New compensation:** a second trial would carry a defined, collectible fee, with a practical way for counsel to continue or withdraw. That would weaken the financial explanation for avoiding retrial.
- **Unpaid work:** a second trial would require substantial additional work without materially new compensation. A mistrial would then extend the prosecution at counsel’s expense.

If another trial meant substantial unpaid work, a remedy that benefited me could cost counsel.

## III. Trial Choices Before Closing {#iii-trial-choices-before-the-shock}

### Opening

By trial preparation, my working relationship with counsel had deteriorated. He continued pressing for a plea despite my repeated refusals and advised against including the Hartmann death threat in my testimony. During preparation, he told me he was counting on me looking guilty.

Counsel opened with words to the effect of:

> “Maybe he did it.”

The transcript controls the exact language and context. Counsel introduced the possibility of my guilt before testing the State’s witnesses.

### The complaining tax official

The complaining tax official supplied the state's account of the unrecorded booth encounter at the center of the prosecution. No neutral witness was inside the booth. Kwak's charging accusation about what I said was false. The encounter involved a demand for about seven times what I owed, escalating pressure inside a tiny enclosed booth, and my frantic attempt to surrender property and expose what was happening.

Retained Trial Counsel's cross-examination was cursory and did little or nothing to test the central dispute.

At trial, Young Kwak testified, in substance, that he merely told me which form to fill out. **That did not happen.** I immediately told Retained Trial Counsel that Kwak had lied and that no such exchange had occurred. Counsel replied, in substance, that the point was not material and was not perjury. The transcript can establish Kwak's exact words.

<span id="excluding-the-accusation-and-testing-the-account"></span>

Kwak's earlier account was presented to the grand jury that indicted me. By trial, the State's presentation had changed. I had already denied the accusation to counsel. The State did not put it before the trial jury or play the recordings of my distress. Kwak instead described a routine exchange about a form. [The Hypothetical Gun](/hawaii-courts/the-hypothetical-gun/#what-did-not-reach-the-trial-jury) compares the accounts in detail. I have not confirmed whether the separate distress recordings were played to the grand jury.

Keeping that material from the trial jury could have had a tactical benefit. That decision did not resolve how counsel should challenge the false form account I had just identified.

To me, counsel let the earlier account disappear without seriously testing the routine exchange Kwak described in its place. Comparing Kwak's prior statements and checking when the form detail first appeared could have tested his credibility. Counsel could also have examined the payment demand and sequence, then sought a ruling outside the jury's presence before deciding how to use an inconsistency or recording. The court might have limited or excluded it; selective use or redaction was not guaranteed.

Before trial, I had asked counsel to obtain a photograph of the actual booth for use as an exhibit. He did not obtain or use it. The booth was tiny, enclosed, and claustrophobic. The State's diagram made it look substantially larger and more comfortable. The jury saw that diagram without the photograph I had asked counsel to obtain.

In *State v. Tetu*, decided in December 2016 before my trial, the Hawaiʻi Supreme Court recognized reasonable defense investigation as part of effective assistance. The case concerned access to a crime scene and discussed photographs and measurements.[^11] It supports examining how counsel investigated the booth’s dimensions and the State’s diagram. It does not establish that he had to obtain the particular photograph I requested.

Counsel's answer about perjury left the credibility problem unanswered. Hawaiʻi law in 2017 also had a separate false-swearing offense for knowingly false sworn statements in official proceedings, without the materiality required for felony perjury. Whether it applied depends on its other elements, including whether the speaker believed the statement was true. [The Hypothetical Gun](/hawaii-courts/the-hypothetical-gun/#materiality-false-swearing-and-witness-credibility) sets out the statutory and evidentiary analysis.

I want the file and trial record to answer these questions.

1. Did counsel compare Kwak's testimony with his earliest complaint, grand-jury testimony, investigator interviews, and other prior accounts, and what did he do with the differences between the charging and trial presentations?
2. What investigation followed my immediate statement that the form testimony was false?
3. Was the statement tested through impeachment or credibility cross-examination?
4. What legal analysis supported counsel's response that the point was immaterial and not perjury?
5. Did counsel inspect the booth or assign anyone to photograph or measure it?
6. Why was my requested photograph not obtained?
7. Did counsel challenge the diagram's foundation, accuracy, scale, or capacity to mislead?
8. What tactical reason, if any, did counsel document for not obtaining the requested photograph, and what other steps did he take to challenge the State's diagram or explain its limits?

Rules 1.1 and 1.3 require reasonably necessary preparation and reasonable diligence.[^13] Those duties apply to counsel's investigation of the witness and the booth.

Under *Briones v. State*, an ineffective-assistance claim requires specific errors or omissions reflecting deficient skill, judgment, or diligence that withdrew or substantially impaired a potentially meritorious defense. The court considers their possible rather than probable effect and respects choices with an obvious tactical basis benefiting the defendant.[^2] An obvious tactical benefit can protect the decision to keep damaging material out. It does not automatically justify failing to investigate and pursue safer ways to test the State's case. In *Aplaca*, the court rejected a strategy explanation that lacked adequate investigation.[^12]

My case ended in a hung jury and no retrial. This review concerns his professional performance, not a pending post-conviction claim.

The transcript can show what counsel asked, how extensively he questioned Kwak, and whether he tested the words attributed to me, the payment calculation, the booth circumstances, the witness's prior accounts, and the physical sequence.

### My testimony and counsel's advice {#my-testimony-and-state-of-mind}

I testified at the July 2017 trial. While I was under indictment and investigating matters for my defense, I intended to disclose what I was uncovering. That included the Hartmann episode and the surrounding pressure history described in [Before the Tax Office](/disclosures/before-the-tax-office/) and [my report to appointed counsel](/hawaii-courts/the-threat-report/).

I told Retained Trial Counsel about the Hartmann death threat. He did not take it seriously and advised me against including it in my trial testimony. I followed that advice and did not testify about it. What he did to assess the report before advising me is separate from whether the testimony would have been admissible. *Aplaca* supports examining the inquiry behind that decision.[^12] His file and account could show what he investigated, what he considered, and what he explained. By trial, Kevin’s warning was also still affecting my willingness to disclose it, as described in [Before the Tax Office](/disclosures/before-the-tax-office/#may-or-june-2017-the-career-warning).

I assess that advice together with his handling of Kwak and the booth evidence. The separate witness-intimidation and extortion questions appear in the [primary account](/hawaii-courts/the-threat-report/#the-legal-questions).

Then Kanemoto gave his closing argument, and the judge called counsel into chambers.

## IV. The Mock Pistol and the Chambers Turning Point

After Kanemoto’s mock-pistol closing, the judge immediately called counsel into chambers. [The Hypothetical Gun](/hawaii-courts/the-hypothetical-gun/) describes Kanemoto’s words and gesture. Here I examine counsel’s response and the plea pressure that followed.

No mistrial motion was made in my presence.

I recall the judge instructing the jury to disregard the mock-pistol episode. [The Hypothetical Gun](/hawaii-courts/the-hypothetical-gun/#the-judge-called-chambers) examines that response and the prejudice standard. The transcript should establish the instruction's wording and timing.

That jury was still deciding whether to convict me of a felony. The argument raised a concrete question for counsel: was the instruction sufficient, or should he seek a mistrial or further relief? *State v. Lagat* places a mistrial ruling within the trial court's discretion.[^15] A request could have been denied; if granted, it could have led to another trial. I needed counsel to explain those options and the consequences of continuing before that jury. His file and the trial record could show how he assessed the instruction, what relief he considered or sought, and what he explained to me.

My later insistence that the jury finish does not establish what counsel had explained after chambers or whether I knowingly chose to forgo any available relief.

The judge, prosecutor, and defense lawyer all saw the closing. The judge’s immediate response made clear that it required discussion.

The lawyers knew more than I did about mistrial standards, preservation, curative remedies, appellate consequences, professional-responsibility exposure, bargaining leverage, and the consequences of retrying a completed felony case.

I saw the gesture and the judge’s reaction. I depended on counsel to explain what they meant for my legal position.

The transcript can establish Kanemoto's words, any objection, interruption or recess, reference to chambers, and jury instruction. It may say nothing about his hand gesture. A recording, contemporaneous notes, or another witness could support or contradict my observation of it.

ABA Defense Function Standard 4-1.5 calls for a clear record for review.[^22] What steps did counsel take to preserve the closing and the court's response?

Counsel's file could show which options were discussed in chambers and immediately afterward:

- objection and preservation;
- curative instruction;
- mistrial;
- putting the incident on the record outside the jury's presence;
- use of the episode in post-trial or appellate proceedings;
- any professional-conduct implications counsel considered; and
- plea negotiations at that time.

I needed to understand my options after the closing before deciding whether to accept the plea counsel pressed during deliberations instead of waiting for the jury's verdict.

## V. Client-Owned Leverage, Lawyer-Controlled Information

The mock-pistol event belonged to my case. Any leverage created by it belonged to the client.

I depended on counsel to explain how the closing affected my options. A personal interest in ending the case could affect both his choice of remedies and the advice he gave me.

### Choice of Remedies {#strategy-distortion}

Did a personal interest in ending the case affect counsel's decision to seek or forgo a mistrial, preservation, or other relief?

If retrial meant substantial unpaid work, counsel’s financial interest could conflict with mine. A successful mistrial request could take the case away from the jury that had seen the mock pistol and require counsel to try it again. An accepted petty-misdemeanor plea would end the case. His retainer and file could show whether the cost of retrial affected the remedies he pursued or the information and advice he gave me.

### Advice to the Client {#potential-information-distortion}

If counsel had that interest, did it affect what he told me about the mock-pistol event and my alternatives before pressing a plea?

Rule 1.4 requires enough information for the client to make informed decisions. Its commentary says a lawyer may not withhold information to serve the lawyer's own interest or convenience, or another person's interests.[^3]

I needed counsel to explain the significance of the closing, the available remedies, and what I would gain or give up under each option. He could recommend a course; I controlled whether to accept a plea.

“Client-owned leverage” describes the client-lawyer relationship in agency theory; it claims no separate legal property right. My authority over a plea had little practical value without enough information about the leverage I would give up.

If counsel's advice failed me, finding and paying for independent advice would fall to me while the case was still underway.

### The Repeat-Player Problem

I bore the criminal consequences while the lawyers continued working in the same courts. Professional goodwill and avoiding friction can have value without a payment or bargain. But ordinary professional contact does not establish a Rule 1.7 conflict. That requires evidence of a particular interest that materially limited counsel’s judgment. The retainer and expected cost of retrial provide the more concrete inquiry here.

Marc Galanter's repeat-player framework explains how continuing participants accumulate experience, relationships, and strategic information unavailable to a person facing one prosecution. A lawyer's interest in future work and institutional goodwill can differ from a client's interest in contesting the prosecution.[^16] The retainer, client communications, chambers accounts, and anticipated retrial costs could show whether those interests materially affected counsel's advice or choice of remedies.

## VI. The Petty-Misdemeanor Offer as an Observable Concession

During deliberations, counsel presented a **petty-misdemeanor** plea to me. The State was asking the jury for a felony conviction. The records need to show whether the offer already existed on the same terms or materially changed at this stage.

The offer’s timing matters:

> **When exactly did the petty-misdemeanor offer originate or materially improve, and did the change follow the mock-pistol closing and chambers conference?**

The records could distinguish three possibilities:

- **P1 — Preexisting and unchanged:** the petty-misdemeanor offer already existed on the same terms and remained actively available before the closing.
- **P2 — Preexisting but revived:** the offer existed earlier but was revived or newly emphasized after chambers.
- **P3 — New or materially improved:** the offer originated or materially improved after chambers.

P1 would weigh against the claim that the closing caused the concession. P2 would show that an existing offer was pressed again after chambers. P3 would require comparing the new or improved terms with what happened in chambers. Timing alone would not explain why the offer changed.

### Ordinary plea-bargaining model

Under this explanation, the offer existed before the closing or changed because of ordinary concerns about the verdict, unrelated to chambers. Counsel conveyed a favorable plea and strongly recommended it to protect me.

### Post-closing scrutiny model {#containment-model}

The judge called counsel into chambers immediately after the mock-pistol event. Under the post-closing scrutiny model, the lawyers understood that event to carry possible mistrial, preservation, professional-responsibility, or other remedial exposure.

Counsel pressed the petty-misdemeanor plea as a way to end the case. The records could show whether it had remained available unchanged, was revived after chambers, or was newly created or materially improved then. Acceptance would end the trial, remove mistrial and retrial risk, and reduce both the need to litigate the closing and its future importance.

For counsel, this explanation turns on whether ending the case served a personal interest. A plea ends the representation at the moment a mistrial could extend it into another felony trial.

This explanation requires no express side agreement. The participants could recognize that the closing required immediate discussion and favor the same outcome for different reasons.

Counsel could not enter a plea for me.

## VII. The Veto Player on the Courthouse Bench

I rejected the petty-misdemeanor offer. I wanted the jury to finish.

Rule 1.2(a) put that decision in my hands.[^1]

Counsel kept pressing.

The confrontation moved to a courthouse bench while the jury deliberated. I was praying. Counsel scolded me, saying in substance that he did not **“believe in that religion stuff”**, while continuing to push the plea.

I had already refused the plea. Counsel kept trying to reverse my decision while twelve jurors were deciding the case. ABA Defense Function Standard 4-5.1(f) cautions against undue influence on a client's plea decision.[^21] What counsel said, the advice he gave, and the circumstances of that pressure matter when assessing whether he crossed that line.

The prosecutor could offer reduced charges and counsel could recommend acceptance. A plea could also avoid the cost and uncertainty of another trial. But it required my consent.

Counsel’s continued pressure showed how strongly he wanted me to accept. His file could help establish whether he was acting solely to protect me, whether his own interest in ending the case affected his advice, or both. The judge’s reasons for any decisions in chambers require a separate account.

Counsel's notes and any courthouse witnesses could establish what he recorded, what I said, and how he continued the discussion after I refused.

### The Appellate Consequence He Did Not Explain

Counsel never explained that accepting an ordinary, unconditional guilty or no-contest plea would generally prevent me from appealing Kanemoto’s earlier closing-argument misconduct.

In *State v. Morin* (1990), the Hawaiʻi Supreme Court held that a voluntary, intelligent guilty plea generally bars later appeals of nonjurisdictional claims, including constitutional challenges to earlier proceedings. A no-contest plea has the same effect. *State v. Kealaiki* (2001) reaffirmed that rule.[^19] Applied here, an ordinary unconditional plea would generally have foreclosed direct appellate review of the alleged closing-argument misconduct. That consequence follows from the valid plea itself; it does not depend on a separate appeal-waiver clause.

The plea counsel pressed therefore carried a cost beyond accepting a petty-misdemeanor conviction. I would ordinarily have surrendered a way to challenge the prosecutor’s conduct if the case otherwise ended in a conviction I could appeal. Counsel kept urging me to plead without explaining that cost.

Rule 1.4(b), in force at the time, required counsel to explain matters sufficiently for me to make informed decisions about the representation.[^20] Whether his advice met that duty must be assessed in the circumstances: I had just witnessed the closing, the judge had called counsel into chambers, and I was being pressed to end the trial while the jury deliberated. His file should show whether he assessed the waiver and what advice he recorded.

Rule 11(a)(2) permits conditional pleas with the court’s approval and the State’s consent, reserving specified adverse pretrial motion rulings in writing.[^19] That provision does not supply an ordinary means of reserving misconduct that occurred during closing argument.

A plea would not have erased the trial record or, by itself, barred a disciplinary complaint. Nor does refusing a plea guarantee appellate review: my jury hung, and the State did not retry me, so there was no conviction to appeal. Challenges to the validity of a plea also stand on a different footing from an appeal of earlier trial errors. The lost opportunity at issue is direct appellate review of the closing, not every possible means of seeking accountability.

This omission belongs alongside the article’s questions about preparation, the fee agreement, and counsel’s response after chambers. The plea he urged would ordinarily have closed that appellate route without his explaining it to me. Whether protecting Kanemoto or avoiding further work influenced his advice remains a separate question requiring evidence of counsel’s reasons.

## VIII. The Hung Jury and Expected Retrial {#viii-the-hung-jury-as-a-revealing-outcome}

Counsel closed with a broad political argument against government power. The jury hung. That outcome did not establish that counsel's preparation and presentation were adequate, or that Kanemoto's mock-pistol argument was harmless. The investigation concerns what counsel presented, what he failed to challenge, and what the court and counsel did in response to the prosecutor's closing. Those decisions remain subject to scrutiny regardless of the jury's inability to reach a verdict.

Counsel did not know the jury would hang when he went into chambers after the closing. The eventual outcome cannot explain what relief he considered then, what he told me, or whether a personal interest affected his advice or his decision to seek or forgo relief while a felony conviction remained possible.

A plea would have ended the case. The hung jury prevented a felony conviction and preserved my claim of innocence. The prosecutor had no conviction and had to decide whether to retry; the case remained unresolved for the judge, and counsel’s representation continued.

Immediately afterward, counsel told me, in substance, **there would be another trial**.

About a day later, counsel called again sounding pleased and reported that the prosecutor was **“spooked”** and would not retry the case.

Counsel expected another trial and sounded pleased when it was no longer going to happen. His reaction alone cannot distinguish relief for me from a personal interest in ending the case. The retainer and his file could help answer that question.

Rule 8.3(a) required counsel to inform the appropriate professional authority if he knew of another lawyer’s rules violation raising a substantial question about honesty, trustworthiness, or fitness. Rule 8.3(c) excludes information protected by Rule 1.6.[^14] Did he evaluate what he witnessed against that standard? I do not know whether he made a report. His file could show his assessment, any confidentiality issue, and any reporting or consultation.

Retained Trial Counsel did not advise me that I could file a disciplinary complaint concerning Kanemoto’s mock-pistol conduct. The trial record could separately establish whether he preserved the incident for appeal. The prosecution ended without a public adjudication of the professional-conduct question.

The ABA Criminal Justice Section's 2023 *Plea Bargain Task Force Report* describes trials and pre- and post-trial litigation as essential to public accountability. It warns that police and government misconduct can go unchecked when cases end in pleas before hearings where that conduct could be examined. It also recommends recording the history of plea offers.[^18] The offer history and counsel's file could show which remedies he considered after Kanemoto's closing and what he expected a plea to resolve.

Kanemoto later denied making the gesture when I confronted him in 2019. That is a disputed factual account, not independent support for a motive to avoid scrutiny. The trial record and other observers could test the gesture; decision records would be needed to establish its effect on plea or retrial decisions.

## IX. Hawaiʻi Conflict Law and Counsel’s Decisions {#ix-hawaiʻi-conflict-law-points-to-the-decision-node}

Having a financial interest in legal work is ordinary. I am asking whether counsel's own interest affected an important decision about my defense.

### *State v. Harter*

In its 2014 decision in *State v. Harter*, the Hawaiʻi Supreme Court examined a lawyer whose professional self-protection conflicted with his client's representation.[^4] Drawing on Rule 1.7,[^5] the court asked whether a personal interest could interfere with independent professional judgment or **foreclose courses of action that reasonably should be pursued for the client**. *Harter* involved different facts, but supplies the standard for examining whether counsel's own interest kept him from pursuing an available course for my defense.

Applied to counsel's preparation, remedies, and advice, *Harter* supports asking:

> **Did any personal interest in avoiding continued litigation, if established, interfere with independent judgment by limiting counsel's preparation or causing him to forgo or inadequately pursue mistrial, preservation, or other relief that reasonably should have been pursued for me?**

### *United States v. Walter-Eze*

The Ninth Circuit asks what plausible alternative strategy went unpursued and whether a competing interest drove that choice.[^6] The theory requires a specific financial interest conflicting with a course that could benefit the client; an ordinary desire to earn money is not enough. Here, the alternatives include mistrial, curative relief, preservation, putting the incident on the record, and informed plea advice.

### *United States v. Hearst*

In *Hearst*, the government and defense lawyer supplied plausible tactical explanations for decisions challenged as financially influenced. The Ninth Circuit still required examination of the alleged financial conflict.[^7] A plausible tactical explanation does not, by itself, resolve the question of a competing financial interest.

### *People v. Barboza* and *Winkler v. Keane*

The California Supreme Court in *Barboza* invalidated a public-defense arrangement that financially penalized the defender for identifying conflicts requiring outside counsel.[^8] The Second Circuit in *Winkler* found an actual conflict where a criminal defense fee created an economic incentive against pursuing plea or lesser-offense alternatives.[^9]

The facts in *Walter-Eze*, *Hearst*, *Barboza*, and *Winkler* differ from mine. I rely on them to examine whether a lawyer's financial interest can impair independent judgment about a particular defense decision.

## X. Three Models for the 2017 Endgame

The explanations below concern particular decisions after the closing. Different decisions may have different causes; no model is established by the sequence alone.

### Model A — Client-centered strategy

Under this explanation, counsel thought the prosecutor's closing did not warrant a mistrial and the petty-misdemeanor plea was overwhelmingly favorable. He pressed it to protect me. The offer existed or improved because of ordinary trial risks. His notes or account would explain why he rejected mistrial or other relief for my benefit and how he explained the plea’s consequences.

A client-centered explanation must also account for counsel's investigation before closing, his examination of witnesses, his exhibit choices, and his advice about my testimony. His reason for pressing the plea cannot alone explain those earlier decisions.

### Model B — Finality-biased representation

If another trial meant substantial unpaid work or other financial costs to counsel, he would have a personal interest in ending the case. This explanation requires evidence that the prospect affected his decisions after the closing or how strongly he pressed the plea.

If counsel limited preparation to save time or expense, that could connect his earlier choices to his later preference for ending the case. The missing photograph, limited cross-examination, and “tight trial” remark do not by themselves establish that motive. His preparation records, retainer, and account could help answer it.

### Model C — Avoiding further scrutiny of the closing {#model-c--tacit-post-closing-containment}

The judge called counsel into chambers immediately after the mock-pistol event. Under this model, the professionals understood the event to create remedial or professional exposure and favored a plea that would end the case. The petty-misdemeanor disposition was being presented or pressed as that solution; its source and timing remain the key records questions. My refusal blocked it.

A separate decision followed the hung jury: whether the State would retry. This branch asks who authorized the decision against retrial, what Kanemoto recommended, and whether concern about scrutiny of the closing influenced it. A plea would have produced a conviction; declining retrial left the State without one. [The Information Trail](/hawaii-courts/information-trail-2015-2022/#the-veto-player) develops that working theory. Counsel's remedy assessment, advice, and continued plea pressure require their own examination for any influence of professional or personal interests. The theory does not establish an agreement among the participants.

These explanations can overlap, but each proposed motive requires its own support. Ending a prosecution does not by itself establish an intent to prevent review.
