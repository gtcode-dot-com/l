Audrey L.E. Stanley[^1] was my assigned public defender when I reported the Hartmann death threat. She dismissed my report and later relayed a tentative proposal involving my departure from Hawaiʻi.

## A Death Threat During My Defense Investigation {#a-death-threat-to-stop-my-defense-investigation}

I knew the Hartmanns through the Haleʻiwa duplex where I rented the back unit. They occupied the front unit.

The threat occurred after February 2016 and before the July 2017 trial. I was under indictment and investigating matters for my defense. I intended to testify about what I uncovered, and Gene Hartmann knew of the indictment and investigation. During an in-person social encounter, Gene and Rita threatened me with death. They delivered the words calmly and quietly:

> **Gene:** “Stay away from [name withheld].”
>
> **Rita:** “Or you will get whacked.”

In context, I understood the warning to target my defense investigation and disclosure. “Stay away” was the instruction; “whacked” was the threatened consequence.

The name in the quotation is withheld. I understood it to refer to a public figure. The allegation concerns what Gene and Rita Hartmann said to me. Their use of the name does not establish that the person issued, authorized, knew of, or participated in the threat.

The following day, Gene reframed the discussion as a “murder mystery.” I told him I would tell everyone. I understood him to be recasting the threat as a harmless conversation.

The housing, room-inquiry, and private-message history appears in [Before the Tax Office](/disclosures/before-the-tax-office/). It explains how these people and conversations entered my life. It does not turn a room applicant, a reference, a relative, or a public figure into a participant in the threat.

### The Legal Questions

Hawaiʻi law raises two questions about that account. HRS §710-1071 addresses threats directed at a witness or someone the speaker believes is about to be called as a witness, with intent to influence testimony. Section 707-764(2) concerns intentionally using specified threats to make someone stop conduct they have a legal right to pursue. Threatened future bodily injury is one of those means. These provisions were in force during the threat period.[^3][^8]

The witness-intimidation question requires evidence of what each speaker understood about my expected testimony and what the warning was intended to accomplish. My intention to testify and Gene’s knowledge of the case and investigation are part of my account. They do not, by themselves, establish either speaker’s understanding of my prospective testimony. *State v. Pudiquet* shows how circumstances can supply evidence of that belief and intent. *State v. Alston* distinguishes the intent elements of witness intimidation and terroristic threatening.[^4]

The extortion question concerns whether the warning was used to make me abandon lawful investigation or disclosure I was pursuing in connection with my defense. It does not depend on prospective-witness status. The surrounding conversation, each speaker’s intent, my legal right to pursue the conduct, and whether the warning compelled or induced me to stop it still need examination.[^9] No criminal adjudication of the Hartmann conduct is reported here.

## What Stanley Heard From Me

I reported the threat to Stanley while she was representing me. I began explaining who had spoken, the condition attached to the warning, and why I took it seriously. She interrupted and dismissed the report, in substance, as “no big deal.” She did not explain how the report would be documented or addressed, how it would affect preparation for my testimony, or what reporting or protective options were available. She also called the Hartmann warning a “conditional death threat.”

Separately, while we reviewed the grand-jury transcript, Stanley laughed at its account of my distress during the tax-office encounter. I was under indictment over that encounter.

## The Leave-Hawaiʻi Communication

Not long after that report, Stanley told me there might be a way to resolve the case if I left Hawaiʻi. She presented it as a tentative idea.

She made clear that the idea was not hers and came from the prosecution. I cannot identify the specific person who originated it. I received no written proposal. Her explanation did not let me distinguish a formal offer from an informal discussion or an idea that had not become an offer.

Rule 1.4(b) required enough explanation for an informed decision about the representation. If Stanley received a proffered plea bargain, Rule 1.4(a)(6) also required prompt communication.[^2] *Missouri v. Frye* becomes relevant if the communication was a formal prosecution plea offer.[^7] Any correspondence, notes, and testimony could establish who within the prosecution proposed departure, how the idea reached Stanley, what leaving required, what would happen to the case, and what advice she gave about accepting or refusing.

## Pressure to Leave {#the-convergence-i-am-identifying}

The Hartmanns separately told me to go away or leave. The concept Stanley relayed from the prosecution also involved leaving Hawaiʻi. Both communications pressed me toward departure. I did not observe coordination between her and the Hartmanns, and I cannot identify the specific person who originated the concept.

Leaving Hawaiʻi would not address the death threat or its effect on my defense investigation and intended testimony. I brought the report to appointed counsel. The next possible resolution she described centered on my departure, without an explanation of how she had handled the report.

## The Wider Pressure Before Trial

In November 2015, James Yuen stood beside a man identified to me as a New Jersey State Police organized-crime investigator when that man invoked my childhood associates and told me he would give me a “colonoscopy.” I understood it as a threat of invasive investigation and use of my past against me. [Before the Tax Office](/disclosures/before-the-tax-office/#november-2015-yuen-and-the-colonoscopy-threat) gives the encounter and the records needed to establish the investigator’s identity and assignment.

In May or June 2017, shortly before trial, “Kevin” threatened career destruction if I continued talking about the Hartmann episode and related conduct. He approached me outside Kahala Whole Foods and invoked a prominent technology investor’s name. His use of the name does not establish the investor’s direction, approval, or prior knowledge. [The chronology](/disclosures/before-the-tax-office/#may-or-june-2017-the-career-warning) records his description of his associations and what he said.

That warning pressured me to stop disclosing what had happened before trial. I do not claim Stanley knew of the Whole Foods encounter.

## What the Representation Required

Hawaiʻi’s professional-conduct rules require competent representation under Rule 1.1, diligence under Rule 1.3, and communication sufficient for informed client decisions under Rule 1.4.[^2] They required her to assess information bearing on my defense, investigate where appropriate, and give me enough explanation to make informed decisions.

In *State v. Aplaca*, the Hawaiʻi Supreme Court explained that an informed decision about prospective defense witnesses requires adequate inquiry into the underlying facts. *State v. Tetu* explains the broader right to reasonable defense investigation.[^5] These authorities support examining the basis for counsel’s decision here. The threat’s relevance to the defense and the appropriate scope of investigation remain questions for the file and the participants.

HRS §802-7 provides for investigatory and other services necessary for an adequate defense, including payment or authorization by the Public Defender under the statute’s conditions.[^6] Was the threat evaluated for defense relevance, and was any investigative assistance considered or used?

I do not claim she had an automatic duty to report the speakers to police. No adjudicated violation is reported here.

A conflict claim would require evidence of an interest that limited her representation and how it affected her work. Her employment, later judicial service, or social connections do not establish that by themselves.
