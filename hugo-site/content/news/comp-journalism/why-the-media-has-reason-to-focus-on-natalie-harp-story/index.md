Perhaps the most publicized name on the national political scene over the past couple of weeks has been Natalie Harp, the White House aide who never seems more than a few steps away from President Donald Trump.

And there’s a good reason for all the attention and coverage she is receiving.

Conversation about Harp picked up recently when it was learned that she was one of the few who joined Trump as he snuck off Air Force One onto another plane because of security concerns following a NATO summit in Turkey.

Then Georgia Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff made a crack about Trump, saying, “See, he doesn’t want to do the job; he wants to build his ballroom and travel with Natalie to their apparently defenseless flying palace gifted by the Emir of Qatar.”

Apparently, Ossoff’s comment struck a nerve. Trump supporters quickly rushed to defend Trump and Harp in a reaction that Ossoff has since called a “White House meltdown.”

It’s true. The right’s overreaction to an off-handed comment does make you wonder why they were so triggered.

But there has been more reporting in recent days about Harp, including coverage of a pair of cringy letters Harp reportedly sent to Trump. After a video showed Harp running behind Trump’s golf cart during a golf outing in Scotland, Harp wrote, “I also am sorry if I was an embarrassment walking the course in Scotland.”

She went on to write, “I want things to always be right between us. I also know I’ve been distracted all week (forgetting to eat throughout the days, and even forgetting to sleep, and only catching a couple hours at a time).”

She signed the letter, “With all my heart, Natalie.”

Over the weekend,
[CNN’s Isabelle Chapman and Andrew Kaczynski reported](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/22/politics/deleted-tweets-trump-aide-natalie-harp-jan-6-invs)

that Harp “flooded her Twitter feed with posts demonstrating extraordinary devotion to (Trump) on January 6, 2021.”

This would have been before Harp worked for Trump. Her X account has since been deleted, but CNN found that Harp posted more than 150 tweets on Jan. 6, 2021. They mostly urged Republicans to “FIGHT FOR TRUMP.” In one tweet alone, Harp wrote “FIGHT FOR TRUMP” 12 times, followed by an American flag and concluding with “Today DC is TRUMP COUNTRY.”

She also live tweeted during Trump’s speech that day, writing, “We will never give up. We will never concede!”

After Joe Biden was certified as president by Congress, Harp tweeted, “America may not be America anymore. But God’s still God. Don’t stop believing!!”

Like Trump, Harp believes the 2020 election was stolen even though there is no proof of widespread voting irregularities.

Why does any of this matter? Because it appears that Harp — a 33-year-old former far-right cable host — might have some influence with Trump.

[The New York Times’ Shawn McCreesh and Maggie Haberman reported](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/18/us/politics/natalie-harp-trump-devotion-white-house.html)

last week, “As long as Ms. Harp is around, he enjoys an unvetted flow of information, some of it poisonous and counterproductive, at least as far as a number of his other advisers are concerned. She supplies him with content she fishes out of the social media gutter (like the video he posted depicting the Obamas as apes) and provides him with a view from the world outside official Washington, of which he has always been mistrustful. Her moniker is ‘the human printer,’ because she tails the president with a portable printer so she can hand him information in hard copy. She is a platinum blond geyser of positivity, there to buck him up when the going gets rough.”

[The Atlantic’s Jonathan Lemire wrote](https://www.theatlantic.com/politics/2026/08/ossoff-trump-natalie-harp/688365/)

, “Each item she prints, whether a positive poll of dubious origin or an attack on a political foe, reinforces Trump’s worldview.”

Haberman has referred to Harp as Trump’s “human binkie.”

So why such devotion from Harp? She credits Trump with saving her life.

The Times wrote, “She has said that she suffered from bone cancer and that it was legislation Mr. Trump signed in 2018 that provided her access to experimental treatments. (She has not publicly detailed which treatments.) She was recounting that experience on Fox News in 2019 when she caught the president’s eye. He invited her to speak at the Republican National Convention in 2020. She spent the next two years repeating his election lies as an anchor on the far-right One America News Network. In 2022, she finally joined his team.”

# **Trump vs. the BBC**

Trump is suing the BBC for defamation, stemming from a documentary that spliced together two parts of his Jan. 6, 2021 speech. The documentary aired a week before the 2024 election — and the editing scandal led to the BBC apologizing to Trump and the resignations of BBC Director-General Tim Davie and news chief Deborah Turness.

Trump is asking for $10 million even though the documentary didn’t air in the U.S., and Trump won the 2024 election.

Now Trump is trying to block the BBC’s efforts to subpoena some of his family members. In a court filing over the weekend, Trump’s lawyers are asking a federal judge to reject the BBC’s request to get testimony and documents from Trump’s son, Donald Jr., as well as Trump’s daughter, Ivanka, and her husband, Jared Kushner.

Trump’s lawyers argued it was a part of a “sweeping, politically charged discovery campaign that has already been rejected by this Court.”

[The Hill’s Mira Bhakta reported](https://thehill.com/regulation/court-battles/6045359-trump-family-bbc-subpoena-block-lawsuit/?tbref=hp)

, “The BBC’s motion, filed last week, argued Trump’s children ‘have personal knowledge and likely have possession, custody, or control of records’ that would be relevant to this case. The broadcaster claimed previous attempts to deliver subpoenas had been thwarted. Trump’s attorneys confirmed the BBC tried and failed to deliver subpoenas to his daughter, son and Kusher. They added that, in May, the BBC attempted to serve Ivanka Trump and Kushner at their residence before the Secret Service stopped them.”

U.S. District Judge Jeffrey Kuntz, who was nominated to the bench by Trump, did not immediately rule on the case.

# **A topic that carries some weight**

Credit The Washington Post for reporting on something that carries some weight: the weight of the president.

When Trump was sworn in as president for his second term in January of 2025, he was 78 years and 220 days old. That made him the oldest person to be sworn in as president. He turned 80 earlier this summer.

So, naturally, there are going to be questions about his health.

[As The Washington Post’s Dan Diamond wrote on Sunday](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/08/23/doctors-say-trump-weight-poses-significant-health-risk/)

, “President Donald Trump has faced scrutiny for bruising on his hands, swelling in his ankles and apparent drowsiness, among other questions about his health.”

Now there’s another concern.

Diamond noted that when Trump was charged in Georgia in an election interference case, his booking record listed his weight as 215 pounds. But in May of this year, according to a medical report released by the White House, Trump weighed 238 pounds — which was less than two pounds from being classified as “obese.”

Diamond wrote, “Trump’s medical measurements have been long dissected, and it’s unclear where the 2023 weight figure originated. But the president has appeared heavier in photos than he did during his campaign, and the administration said he gained 14 pounds between his most recent annual medical checkups.”

Diamond added, “White House physicians have said they’ve advised Trump — who has made little secret of his love for fast food and his distaste for intense exercise — to focus on weight loss, improving his diet and sustained physical activity. Outside doctors put it more directly: The 80-year-old president would be safer if he reduced his waistline.”

For the record, Diamond wrote, “The White House did not directly respond to questions about Trump’s weight, pointing instead to his energy and stamina.”

# **A rare loss for The New York Times**

![](https://www.poynter.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/NYT-AP-300x200.jpeg)

(Mark Lennihan/AP, File)

Catching up on this story from late last week. For the first time in 50 years, The New York Times lost a defamation lawsuit involving one of its stories.

Kai Spears, who played men’s basketball at the University of Alabama, was awarded $9.25 million in damages after a jury decided the paper defamed him in a 2023 article that incorrectly reported he was at the scene of a deadly shooting in Tuscaloosa, Alabama.

Three other University of Alabama basketball players were present at a shooting that killed a 23-year-old mother. One player was charged with capital murder and awaits trial. Two others were not implicated in the shooting or accused of any wrongdoing.

[The 2023 story](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/03/15/sports/ncaabasketball/alabama-shooting.html)

, written by staffer Billy Witz, now includes a lengthy editor’s note that said, in part, “The original version of this article, published March 15, misidentified the person who was in the car with Brandon Miller when the shooting occurred. Based on information from a person familiar with the case, the article erroneously identified that person as Kai Spears, a freshman basketball player. After the article was initially published, Alabama’s athletic director and Spears’s father denied that Spears was present. The Times included those responses and reviewed its reporting, but did not conclude that any other change to the article was warranted at that time.”

Charlie Stadtlander, a spokesperson for The Times, said in a statement,



“We’re disappointed the jury found The Times liable for an honest mistake. We thank the jury for its service, but believe the verdict and award of damages are contrary to law and not supported by the evidence.”

The Times is considering its legal options.

[The New York Times’ Katie Robertson wrote](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/20/business/media/new-york-times-defamation-lawsuit-alabama.html)

, “The article, by the reporter Billy Witz, said that Mr. Spears had also been present at the shooting, attributing the knowledge to a single anonymous source. The article said that Mr. Spears had declined to comment and that university officials had not immediately responded to a request for comment.”

Robertson would later write, “During the trial, lawyers for The Times argued that the article was not defamatory because it did not convey that Mr. Spears had been involved in the shooting, even if it incorrectly placed him at the scene. They also argued that Mr. Witz had taken reasonable steps to corroborate his source’s information, including approaching Mr. Spears in person. Jurors were shown surveillance footage, dashcam footage and text messages that confirmed Mr. Spears was nearby in another car, but not at the scene. He testified that he was heckled at a game the day after the article was published and had been under so much stress that his grades slipped.”
