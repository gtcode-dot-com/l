In 2024, the Medill Local News Initiative at Northwestern University conducted its first in-depth survey of Chicago-area news consumers to understand how smartphones and social media were remaking the relationship between audiences and local news. Medill did a second survey in 2025. It has now completed a
[third annual study](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/assets/research/medill_chicago_news_survey_2026.pdf)
to capture not just a 2026 snapshot but a three-year timeline of the digital revolution’s impact on local news.

The survey results lay bare the severe pressure faced by legacy media to retain and grow audiences. Print has become a niche product, while linear broadcast TV and radio are losing their audiences at an alarming rate. One encouraging sign is that survey respondents say they maintain a strong interest in consuming local news. However, if news organizations believe that incorporating artificial intelligence is an easy fix for streamlining newsgathering at lower cost, they will need to think hard about their approaches. This year’s study looked closely at the relationship between consumers and AI. It found that respondents are deeply skeptical of AI in news for anything more than helping to check spelling or grammar.

With three years of survey data, it’s clear news consumers have established new habits and tastes in the way they access information. Their device of choice is the smartphone, and they are much more likely to search for news or turn to social media than pay for subscriptions. Young people in particular are reliant on content creators for local news, one of many major changes underway in the way people search out and absorb news and information.

Three years isn’t a lifetime, or even the lifespan of a smartphone. But in terms of how technological and social change are buffeting the news industry, the speed of transformation is forever remaking how people get news and the providers that supply it.

## 2026 survey opinions about AI and news

The 2026 survey asked many of the same questions and explored many of the same issues as the previous reports. But this year’s Medill study focused more attention on one of the latest, most controversial innovations in the news field – the use of artificial intelligence in the news-gathering process.

AI, which offers news organizations the potential to save time and money in news production, is already in use in some newsrooms, though it is still largely at the experimental stage. The Medill survey provides a warning to news leaders. Respondents showed clear distrust of AI in the news-gathering process. Discomfort with AI was widespread. It applied to many facets of newsroom activities, including the use of AI to write stories or create images, deciding which stories to prioritize, personalizing coverage and even summarizing the news.

## Chicago-area as a microcosm for the country

The Medill Local News Initiative at Northwestern University produces groundbreaking research about the digital revolution’s impact on local journalism, with the goal of supporting industry professionals as they reshape news organizations into sustainable, vigorous enterprises. For the third year in a row, the Local News Initiative commissioned a comprehensive, scientific survey of news consumer habits and opinions in the Chicago metropolitan area. This survey of 1,223 adults examined news consumer behavior in Chicagoland, but it can be taken as a microcosm for what is happening locally across the country. The survey included the city, suburbs, exurbs and rural areas on the outskirts of the metro area.

## News events drive engagement

The Medill survey over its three-year existence confirms a positive starting point for assessing the popularity of local news: There is strong interest among Chicago-area residents in local news as well as national news. The majority of Chicago-area adults (54%) follow local news daily, while 85% engage with local news providers at least once a week. Those figures have held steady since 2024. Overall, just 4% of respondents said they never engage with local news.

For comparison’s sake, the survey also asked respondents about their engagement with national news. Chicago-area consumers follow national news even more closely than local news, with 62% consuming national news daily, and 90% consuming national news weekly.

News events undoubtedly drive consumer engagement. Over the past two years, President Donald Trump’s busy second term has generated intense daily interest in national news. There are indications in the 2026 survey that a major national news story with an important local angle created greater interest in local news. Beginning last September, the United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) conducted significant operations in and around Chicago. The activity, known as Operation Midway Blitz, focused heavily on the region’s Hispanic population. Local news organizations covered events closely. The Medill 2026 survey did not specifically ask about Operation Midway Blitz, but it likely tracked heightened interest in this story at the level of demographic differences, with 53% of Hispanic respondents consuming local news daily, compared to 45% in 2025.

## Broadcast news loses traction

The 2024 Medill survey showed how small print newspaper audiences have become: Just 13% of respondents said they read a print newspaper or magazine often or all the time. That compared to 52% for local television news. In the 2026 survey, the print audience has held steady at 12%, but it’s now broadcast news that is sliding precipitously. According to the survey, the daily audience for television dropped from 52% in 2024 to 47%. The loss of radio audience is even steeper: From 34% to 25%.

These results are not necessarily a surprise. While print news has been in steep decline for two decades, TV news audiences slipped at a slower pace.

But it seemed inevitable that the forces of the digital revolution would catch up with television – and now it’s happening. The disruption of broadcast news is upon us. The trend is particularly strong among younger audiences. While 67% of survey takers in 2026 ages 60+ said they use the television to get local news often or all the time, the number drops to 29% among 18-29 year olds.

The question then becomes where are these audiences going? They have migrated to digital sources. Among the 2026 survey’s results, 67% of respondents rely on their smart phones to consume local news often or all the time (an increase from 62% in 2024). Desktop and laptop usage also increased to 37% from 30% in 2024.

Here is where newspapers have done a better job than television of reaching audiences where they want to be. The survey asked respondents not just about the devices they use to get news but how often they consume local news from a specific source across any medium. This was done to track, for example, whether a consumer read a newspaper’s website or watched TV news segments on social media.

The result indicates that newspapers are far ahead in the critical task of going where the audiences are. For example, of people who reported getting local news at least once a month from a newspaper, 47% said they consumed news from the newspaper’s website, app or email. By contrast, people who reported getting local news at least once a month from TV mainly watched their television sets (62%). Just 15% went to the local television station’s website, app or email. This shows that local TV news still has a long way to go to fix its audience problem.

## The search for good news

The fact that newspaper owners have moved on from print and now reach their audiences on the web is one important starting point for change. There are, in fact, local newspapers around the country that have shifted their focus to digital operations and are financially stable. In every era of journalism, technology has forced change on newsrooms. In that way, this era is no different.

The pressure points are intense, however. The Medill survey looked at the relationship between audiences and their willingness to pay for news and identified a troubling trend. News consumers don’t want to pay for news, and many believe that no one should pay. According to the survey, the number of people who have given money to a local news organization by subscribing, donating or becoming a member has dropped from 19% in 2024 to 14% in 2026.

Perhaps the most hopeful signs in the survey are that audiences retain a strong positive relationship with local news. Taken together, the results of the 2026 Medill Survey of Chicago-area news consumers show that audiences are out there, especially on their smart phones. And there are ways to reach even young audiences. For example, 44% of respondents ages 18-29 report consuming local news daily from content creators. In addition, news consumption by this young cohort increased to 43% in 2026 from 32% in 2025.

The news landscape is shifting rapidly in the digital era, but some things don’t change. The challenge facing news organizations for generations has been to deliver timely, useful news and information to audiences when they want it and where they want it. Now news providers must quickly find new ways to do that in a time of profound change.
