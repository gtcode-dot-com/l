As the US 2026 midterm elections approach on Nov. 3, Super PAC spending on political advertising has
[reportedly soared to record highs](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/politics/midterm-elections-political-ad-spending-record-2026/)
, surpassing US$11 billion last week and exceeding the amount spent on
[any other election cycle](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/2026-midterms-become-expensive-election-cycle-ever-rcna605964)
to date.

Super PACs are a particular type of political action committee (PAC) that may receive
[unlimited contributions](https://www.fec.gov/press/resources-journalists/political-action-committees-pacs/)
from individuals, corporations, labour unions and other PACs for the purpose of financing independent expenditures and other independent political activity.

In this year’s elections, LTF’s and PFA’s Super PACs, funded by the AI industry heavyweights, have played an
[outsized role](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/09/03/ai-crypto-super-pac-spending-primaries-01062929)
in supporting or opposing candidates through advertising. But as polls show
[distrust of AI](https://time.com/article/2026/10/21/how-america-s-ai-trust-issues-could-impact-the-midterms/)
[crosses party lines](https://www.cnet.com/news/politics/polls-experts-ai-data-centers-midterm-voting/)
, the advertisements that Silicon Valley’s leaders are
[paying millions of dollars](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/09/29/us/politics/ai-super-pac-money-midterm-elections.html)
to try to sway the results in their chosen candidates’ favour often do not mention AI at all.

*Photo: Reuters/Shannon Stapleton*

While LTF is a registered Super PAC itself, FEC filings show it mainly disburses money to other Super PACs in its network which run political ads.

Of about $52 million that LTF has
[disbursed to date](https://www.fec.gov/data/committee/C00916114/?tab=spending)
, $20 million went to Think Big, which targets the Democratic voter base, and another $20 million went to American Mission, which focuses on Republicans.

PFA, meanwhile, is registered as a “social welfare”, or
[501(c)(4)](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/other-non-profits/social-welfare-organizations)
, organisation. This type of non-profit is
[not required](https://www.opensecrets.org/outside-spending/faq)
to disclose its donors or disbursements. However, PFA
[contributes funds to three Super PACs](https://www.axios.com/2026/02/27/ai-influence-power-players)
: the bipartisan Public First PAC, Jobs and Democracy for Democratic candidates and Defending Our Values for Republicans. FEC filings for these Super PACs show Public First PAC received $1 million,
[Jobs and Democracy](https://www.fec.gov/data/committee/C00928374/?tab=raising)
received $15.4 million and Defending Our Values received $7.9 million from PFA.
[Public First](https://www.fec.gov/data/committee/C00930503/?tab=spending)
PAC’s FEC filings also show that $4 million of its $4.4 million in disbursements disclosed so far went to Jobs and Democracy, while another $300,000 went to Defending Our Values.

*Disbursements to Think Big, American Mission, Jobs and Democracy and Defending Our Values from Leading the Future, Public First PAC and Public First Action. Graphic: Bellingcat*

Super PACs
[are allowed to](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/taking-receipts-pac/contributions-to-super-pacs-and-hybrid-pacs/)
accept unlimited contributions from other political committees, and other similar networks of Super PACs have been observed in the current election cycle, such as cryptocurrency-linked
[Fairshake and its affiliated Super PACs](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/05/fairshake-crypto-super-pac-house-races.html)
.

Only ads supporting or opposing two candidates mentioned AI, and none mentioned data centres, a
[top issue](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/why-data-centers-are-a-top-issue-in-the-2026-midterms/)
this election cycle. A
[*New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/09/29/us/politics/ai-super-pac-money-midterm-elections.html)
analysis of 95 video ads paid for by the same four Super PACs, using data gathered by media tracking firm AdImpact, had similar findings.
[FEC rules](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/candidate-taking-receipts/understanding-independent-expenditures/)
state that Super PACs can spend money to support or oppose a candidate, but cannot coordinate with the candidate or their campaign on this. There is no evidence that any of the candidates or campaigns for which the Super PACs ran ads have coordinated with them.

Michael Beckel, Director of Money in Politics Reform at cross-partisan political reform group Issue One, told Bellingcat that voters are at a disadvantage when special interests fund Super PACs running ads that don’t mention the priority issues of those underwriting them.

“Special interests seem keen on moving voters with messaging around hot-button issues, all while advancing a specific policy agenda that viewers of ads are kept in the dark about,” he said. The result of this, Beckel added, was that “the industry gains a stronger hand in Washington, while voters get little chance to judge the agenda their votes could help advance”.

A representative for PFA stated: “We endorse officials who support regulating AI. The aligned PACs run ads on the issues that matter most immediately to voters in each race, and for most electorates right now that means the economy, affordability, public safety, and other issues that they are facing on a daily basis.”

Jobs and Democracy, Defending Our Values, LTF, Think Big and American Mission did not respond to questions about their political advertising as of publication.

A
[recent survey](https://guardrailsaction.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/GuardrailsActionDFPPollReport.pdf)
by think tank Data for Action and non-profit Guardrails Action found that about three-quarters of likely voters would be less inclined to vote for a candidate if they found out they had accepted a donation from an AI Super PAC.

The ads distributed by the AI-backed Super PACs supporting or opposing 29 candidates mentioned economic issues such as taxes, the cost of living, consumer rights and local jobs or small businesses. Another 16 mentioned immigration or law enforcement and 17 mentioned US President Donald Trump or his “Make America Great Again” (MAGA) movement.

Like the ads mentioning Trump, those on immigration were sharply divided in messaging depending on whether they were targeting Democratic or Republican voters, even when they were paid for by the AI Super PACs in the same network.

LTF’s Think Big sponsored an ad for Democratic candidate Jeremy Moss in Michigan that talks about how he stood up to Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), while American Mission’s ad for Republican Clay Fuller in Georgia mentions how he is focused on securing the southern border.

## Unknown Vendors

Federal campaign finance laws
[require](https://www.fec.gov/introduction-campaign-finance/)
federal political committees to file periodic campaign finance reports disclosing their receipts and disbursements.

Our
[analysis of FEC filings](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1iIMqLdPt55ATUd0mifvpUTil_JY1Ls12VhudggneWj4/edit?usp=sharing)
for LTF and the Super PACs in its network revealed that they were channelling more than $36.4 million in total towards four companies that were newly set up, with no apparent online presence outside of these filings.

A
[complaint](https://campaignlegal.org/sites/default/files/2026-05/AISubvendorComplaintFinal%20%281%29.pdf)
to the FEC by the non-profit Campaign Legal Center (CLC) in May cited American Mission and Think Big’s payments to Summit Ridge and Lantern Productions,
[alleging that](https://campaignlegal.org/document/ai-industry-funded-super-pacs-unlawfully-evaded-transparency-rules-clc-alleges)
the Super PACs had “unlawfully evaded federal reporting requirements by funnelling their payments for goods and services through shell companies set up to conceal how the super PACs are actually spending their money”. The FEC has not yet ruled on the complaint. LTF, American and Think Big did not respond to Bellingcat’s queries about the complaint as of publication.

[FEC filings](https://www.fec.gov/data/committee/C00916114/?tab=spending)
show that LTF paid a company called Prusik Partners LLC more than $966,000 for operational expenditures for a range of services including communications and strategy consulting, as well as legal fees.

The network’s Democrat-focused Super PAC,
[Think Big](https://www.fec.gov/data/committee/C00923417/?tab=spending)
, paid a company called Lantern Production Consultants LLC more than $20 million, based on filings for disbursements from Aug. 20, 2025 to Oct. 14, 2026.

The payments included $3.17 million for operational expenditures for a range of services such as media consulting and research, and another $17 million for
[independent expenditures](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/candidate-taking-receipts/understanding-independent-expenditures/)
. The FEC defines an independent expenditure as one that pays for “a communication that expressly advocates for the election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate”. The highest independent expenditures paid to Lantern ($8 million) were to oppose Bores’ candidacy, the filings show.

Think Big also declared in its filings that it had paid a company called Blue Horizon Compliance Consultants LLC almost $55,700 for “compliance consulting”.

Republican-focused
[American Mission](https://www.fec.gov/data/committee/C00916692/?tab=spending)
, on the other hand, paid a company called Summit Ridge Media LLC more than $13 million, based on filings for disbursements from Aug. 20, 2025 to Oct. 14, 2026.

This included $2.2 million for operating expenditures such as polling and website services, and $10.9 million in independent expenditures, with the highest amount (about $960,400) going towards communications in support of James Morris Kingston, the Republican candidate for the House of Representatives in Georgia.

Bellingcat found that all four of these LLCs listed mailing offices and office supply goods stores in Nevada as their mailing addresses. We could not find records of them doing business with any entity outside of the LTF network – in fact, they appeared to have no online presence aside from their names appearing in the filings and business registrations.

Despite their addresses being listed in Nevada, none of the companies could be found in the state’s
[public business registry](https://orion.nv.gov/portal/public/#/public/nvsos/en/CaseXscreen?screen=external-GenericFilingsSearch&amp;tabRoute=business)
. Instead, Bellingcat found registrations for Prusik, Lantern Production and Summit Ridge across the country – in Delaware.

While Delaware is the second-smallest state in the US, more than half of all Fortune 500 companies are incorporated there according to the
[Delaware Secretary of State](https://corp.delaware.gov/stats/)
. The state’s business-friendly laws mean companies can reportedly incorporate
[in minutes](https://www.currentaffairs.org/news/what-the-hell-is-going-on-in-delaware)
and are only required to name a representative – who can be a paid agent unrelated to the business – in public registration documents, not the beneficial owners.

Articles of incorporation from the Delaware Secretary of State showed that Prusik was registered on July 22, 2025, while Lantern Production and Summit Ridge were both registered on Oct. 30, 2025. We also found a Delaware registration for a company called “Blue Horizon Compliance Group LLC”, registered on Oct. 30, 2025. While the name differs slightly from the Blue Horizon Compliance Consultants LLC that Think Big declared payments for, the registration was signed by the same agent listed in the documents for Lantern Production and Summit Ridge.

The filings show that all of these companies – including Blue Horizon Compliance Group, if this was indeed the same entity referred to in Think Big’s filings – began receiving payments from the Super PACs within two to three months of their business registrations.

Saurav Ghosh, CLC’s Director of Federal Campaign Finance Reform, told Bellingcat that the group filed the complaint because “when groups funded by big corporate special interests evade transparency laws, voters lose”.

“Federal reporting requirements help ensure transparency in our elections and protect voters’ legal right to know where the money is going,” Ghosh said. “Secret spending schemes like this make it virtually impossible to ensure that our elected officials are staying accountable to voters instead of wealthy special interests.”

We were unable to reach out to the LLCs as none of them had phone numbers or email addresses listed online. LTF, Think Big, and American Mission did not respond to questions about the LLCs, including whether “Blue Horizon Compliance Consultants LLC” listed in Think Big’s filings was the same company as Delaware-registered “Blue Horizon Compliance Group LLC”. However, a
[WIRED story](https://www.wired.com/story/the-biggest-pro-ai-super-pac-network-has-poured-millions-into-nevada-shell-companies/)
on these same LLCs quoted a spokesperson from LTF as saying that the complaint was “a desperate ploy for attention from Public First, Anthropic, and the groups aligned with their outside organisations”. WIRED also
[quoted](https://www.wired.com/story/the-biggest-pro-ai-super-pac-network-has-poured-millions-into-nevada-shell-companies/)
a Public First spokesperson denying that the group had anything to do with the complaint.

Beckel suggested that stricter transparency measures relating to Super PAC spending would help inform and empower voters. For instance, Congress could pass legislation to require ads to include the names of the top donors, he said, adding that similar rules had been passed with cross-partisan support in places like Alaska and Arizona.

“Congress would be doing voters a huge service by passing more robust transparency requirements for political ads. Voters deserve to know who is behind the ads they’re seeing,” Beckel said.

*Google Ads Analysis*

We reviewed ads available through

For the coding of themes, we looked at whether each of the themes mentioned (AI, data centres, economy, Trump, and immigration or law enforcement) appeared in any of the ads for each candidate. This does not account for how many times each theme may have appeared in ads for a candidate.
