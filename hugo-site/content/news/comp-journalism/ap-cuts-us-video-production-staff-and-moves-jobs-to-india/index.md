The Associated Press will lay off 20 video production staff in the U.S. on Friday and move those jobs to a new hub in India, according to the union representing those journalists.

An additional 10 photo editors in the U.S. and 10 video production staff in London are being targeted for layoffs, though it is unclear when their jobs would be terminated. The decision to eliminate journalists with decades of experience and move their jobs covering American news to India raises concerns about the quality of future coverage, union leaders said.

“They are experts in their region, in their topics, in their abilities to pick and choose what clips should be elevated,” said Kimberlee Kruesi, a breaking news and politics reporter who serves as the acting president of the News Media Guild. “There’s a lot of questions about creating a hub outside of the U.S. where we don’t know if they’re going to be up to speed on all these topics and regions.”

New Media Guild executive director Tony Winton said the union was initially told that staff would be laid off July 31, but AP later pushed that date to Friday for the U.S.-based video producers. The company still has not given the union a date for the photo editors’ layoffs.

Executive editor Julie Pace first announced that the AP would open a new production hub in Noida, India, in an email sent to staff in June. She noted that the initiative would “affect” some U.S. staff and that the company was also “exploring making production changes” in other locations.

“Visuals are incredibly important to AP and its future. To that end, we are planning to develop a centralized global hub in India to increase the size of our production staff to scale both our video and photo capabilities while improving speed and quality,” Pace wrote in the email. She added, “India has a vibrant, digital-first media industry. We have done some hiring in production earlier this year to test the market, and we’ve long had success hiring for other teams there. Other news organizations have been building their presence in India in recent years as well.”

Bloomberg is also expanding its presence in India, where it has grown its staff by 30% in the past three years,
[according to Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/19/bloomberg-new-economy-forum-india)

. Noida, which will be the site of the AP’s new multimedia production hub, is home to many Indian news channels and film studios.

It is unclear how workflows at the AP would be affected. Kruesi said that even though she mostly works with text, she is in regular contact with video and photo staff as part of her job. She added that the company has not provided the union with information about how U.S.-based journalists would be expected to coordinate with those in India.

In an emailed statement, AP spokesperson Patrick Maks reiterated Pace’s statement that the new hub would increase the size of AP’s production staff and its capabilities.

“Over 80% of our output today is visual, and we know those formats are increasingly important to our customers and audiences,” Maks wrote. “As a global news organization, we have staff in nearly 100 countries – including in India, where we’ve long maintained a presence – who play an essential role in delivering fast, accurate and independent journalism from every corner of the world.”

The AP in recent years has pushed to
[increase its video content](https://variety.com/2026/tv/news/associated-press-launches-live-stream-video-shows-1236689992/)

. Since 2022, it has
[doubled](https://apnews.com/article/news-industry-buyouts-ap-newspapers-dd790effc6a385514b3323560161ea4f)

the number of video journalists it employs in the U.S. (Those journalists work in the field and are separate from the video producers affected by the layoffs.)

Kruesi said that after the layoffs Friday, there will still be a “handful” of video production staff left in the U.S., mostly those focusing on specialized beats like religion. But the layoffs will eliminate the video production journalists who currently handle the majority of U.S. news.

The AP is well-known for its photography, for which it has won 36 Pulitzer Prizes. Laying off photo and video staff puts the quality of its coverage at risk, Winton said. Staff members don’t merely edit photos or videos; they must interact with the journalists gathering the images and often apply their knowledge of the subject. Sometimes, they must also gather content themselves, finding user-generated video or photos from government sources. As a result, they must be able to vet images and videos and ensure they are not manipulated.

For example, Winton said, if the AP needs police body camera or dashcam footage, the journalist in charge of obtaining that footage would need to have knowledge of American public records law. They would need to know where and how to get that footage and how to attribute it.

“To the extent that the knowledge and skills of these individuals who’ve been working in many cases for decades would be summarily replaced by a group of individuals who would not be familiar with all of that context, background, and potentially skills — that I think speaks to the risk to quality,” Winton said.

The Friday layoffs will mark the latest in a series of rapid cuts stretching back to 2024, when the company eliminated roughly 8% of its global workforce. Winton said the union has shrunk by about 150 members since then, due to a combination of layoffs and buyouts. Earlier this year, 40 staff members took buyouts and an additional 20 were laid off.
