In interviews, Ruth Pearl would recall a dream she had about her son. She was in what she described as a faraway Middle Eastern city, in an alley somewhere.

“All of a sudden, I see Danny really scared,” Pearl said. “You don’t see Danny scared, because he was very secure and calm.”

Her son, Daniel Pearl, was an acclaimed American journalist for The Wall Street Journal whose work had taken him around the world. At the time, Pearl was based in Mumbai, India, but had also been traveling to Pakistan to report.

When Ruth awoke from the dream, she went to her computer to email Daniel. In the message, she asked that her son humor her and write back.

“I sensed the danger,” she later recalled. “I sensed the moment.”

On Jan. 23, 2002, Daniel Pearl, who was in the port city of Karachi, Pakistan, thought he was meeting a potential source for a story about terrorism. Instead, he was kidnapped and held for a week before being killed. Pearl’s captors forced him to make statements on camera.

Pearl was ultimately beheaded. And a video of Pearl’s killing was distributed online for the world to see.

“I would say just on a personal level, he was obviously such an extraordinary guy and a great reporter,” said

Michael H. Posner, who served as

the United States assistant secretary of state for democracy, human rights and labor in the Obama administration. “

But it really was, to me, a moment where the rules of the game had changed.”

Posner said he grew up in a period

when journalists, the Red Cross and humanitarian workers were afforded some special protection. These groups, he added, were regarded not as combatants or participants, but as legitimate intermediaries: journalists reporting, humanitarians dealing with vulnerable populations, and the Red Cross working between combatants on both sides to mitigate the damage of conflict.

“And all of a sudden,” Posner said, “this was one of those moments where I thought, ‘Oh my God, the world just got less safe. We’re going to have less ability to know what’s going on, and the kind of climate of protection for journalists is now in serious jeopardy.’”

The murder of Pearl, a beloved and well-regarded journalist, sent shockwaves through the journalism industry and around the world.

Congress unanimously passed the U.S. Daniel Pearl Freedom of the Press Act in 2010, requiring the State Department to expand its annual human rights reports by tracking global press freedom abuses. Surrounded by Pearl’s family, President Barack Obama
[said](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pdfzN-Kmao8)

the legislation sent a strong signal about
[“our core values”](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/blog/2010/05/17/protecting-press-freedoms-worldwide)

about freedom of the press.

![](https://www.poynter.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/AP100517020598.jpg)

“There was such outrage over his murder that there was an effort by the Congress, by the U.S. government, to say: ‘We’ve got to basically do whatever we can as a government to protect journalists and to make people aware, if they attack journalists, there’s a price to be paid,’” said Posner, who is now a professor at New York University’s Stern School of Business

and director of the school’s Center for Business and Human Rights. “

There’s a symbolic quality to that.”

Pearl’s murder

and the global outrage that followed prompted a reckoning over the risks journalists face while covering war and terrorism. His death became part of a larger shift toward the protections, training and vigilance that now characterize conflict reporting.

Jeffrey A. Sonnenfeld, a close friend of Pearl’s, said

many younger journalists might believe journalists have always faced this kind of danger. “But in fact, like Red Cross workers in battle zones or Red Crescent workers or things like that, there was some degree of exemption around (the) media.”

Sonnenfeld said journalists had long been inadvertent victims of violence — killed by shrapnel, for example — but weren’t deliberately targeted.

“And I’m not saying there weren’t many heroic journalists, but they weren’t being specifically targeted because they were journalists or representing a nation,”

Sonnenfeld said. “

This was a major turning point, that journalists didn’t have this Swiss-like sense of neutrality about them when they traveled. That they were still identified with either their host country or their institution, or just were attractive bait that somebody could capture to use for negotiation purposes. That hadn’t been the case before.”

Sonnenfeld, the

senior associate dean for leadership studies and Lester Crown professor in management practice at the Yale School of Management, was a professor at Emory University when he first met Pearl. Both were single and Jewish, and both had a wide variety of friends across sectors and backgrounds.

“We liked to challenge the pretenses of our fields — me with academia, and Danny with journalism,”

Sonnenfeld said. “

To not be captured by trying to climb hierarchical ladders, we would often challenge authority.”

Sonnenfeld said he and Pearl both had a good sense of humor, though

his own style was

more overt while Pearl’s was much more subtle, clever and sophisticated.

Born in New Jersey to Judea and Ruth Pearl, Daniel grew up in Los Angeles. He began his journalism career at the Berkshire Eagle in Massachusetts.

Sonnenfeld said Pearl was beloved there and had a way of embedding himself in the communities he covered. That happened in Atlanta, too, which Sonnenfeld described as another huge culture shift from Pearl’s Los Angeles childhood.

“

We would go out, and he’d go into parts of town that I would only go (to) because he told me to go there, but all the advisories were, ‘It’s unsafe.’ But Danny saw people as people. He didn’t care about any of the demographic divides that lead people to fear each other,”

Sonnenfeld

said. “So he would go off and he’d just wander in.”

He said Pearl sometimes brought his basketball and asked to join games.

“They embraced him. They saw he was a good guy, and he saw they were great guys,”

Sonnenfeld recalled. “

He could just get along very well with anybody.”

Sonnenfeld

also said Pearl had a distinctive look and didn’t know that women found him appealing. Men were drawn to his charisma, too. “He was oblivious to it,”

Sonnenfeld said.

Pearl,

Sonnenfeld noted, had a violin

he could play magnificently.

Sonnenfeld recalled that while Pearl was working for

The Wall Street Journal in New York, he would go to blues bars in Greenwich Village after helping to put the paper to bed.

![](https://www.poynter.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/AP081111043865.jpg)

Memorial plaques honoring Wall Street Journal reporters Jeff Cole and Daniel Pearl are displayed in the newspaper’s New York newsroom on Nov. 11, 2008. Pearl was kidnapped and killed in Pakistan in 2002 while reporting on terrorism. (AP Photo/Mark Lennihan)

“And of course he would take along his trusty violin, and he’d just join in with whomever was there,” Sonnenfeld said. “And you have to know that the clientele of the places where he would go would be extremely unlikely that any of them ever read the Wall Street Journal, not only not as regulars, but as infrequent readers. They probably had heard of it, but they didn’t care who he was, and he didn’t care if they knew who he was. In fact, he’d rather they didn’t.”

When news broke that Pearl had been kidnapped, Sonnenfeld and Pearl’s colleagues launched a desperate effort to locate him. Pearl’s wife, Mariane, called John Bussey, his boss at The Wall Street Journal.

“I said, ‘Danny’s not back,’” Mariane Pearl
[recalled](https://www.oprah.com/omagazine/oprah-interviews-mariane-pearl)

to Oprah Winfrey in a 2003 interview.

Mariane, a French journalist, met Daniel in 1998, and they married the following year. She had been drawn to what she described as Daniel’s “talent for life.”

“I admired his work as a journalist. I admired his integrity. I admired his effort to be so open to the world,” Mariane said. “I didn’t know where it came from — he’d grown up as a Jewish boy in LA, an all-American boy, right? But together we traveled the world, and his capacity to just be himself wherever he was fascinated me. And we had something in common: this desire, this appetite for the world.”

But now here Mariane was — pregnant with their first child — in a desperate race against time alongside police, international diplomats and Pearl’s friends and colleagues.

“We knew Danny was in a life-or-death situation. So we were in a fighting mood,” Mariane told Winfrey. “They were trying to scare us, so I couldn’t be scared. That was the first thing.”

Pearl’s close friend and colleague Asra Nomani also tried to help. Daniel and Mariane had been staying at Nomani’s Karachi home when Daniel left for the meeting and never returned.

In a 2021
[podcast interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J8WnUB6TvVY)

, Nomani said she wished a “January 23, 2002 moment” on no one. But in that moment, she tried to summon every bit of courage, capacity and capability she had to save Daniel. She immediately got on the phone with other journalists to find out who Daniel was supposed to meet. She called relatives and sources.

For his part, Sonnenfeld called everyone he could.

“I never liked to believe that I ever anticipated what would happen to him, would really happen to him,” he said. “It was so ridiculous, so worthless, so shameful and cruel.”

Sonnenfeld said it’s important for the public to recognize that these are different times, whether the journalist at risk is Jamal Khashoggi or Evan Gershkovich. The circumstances of their cases were vastly different, but both underscored the reality Pearl’s murder had made impossible to ignore. A journalist’s profession could itself make him a target.

“And these journalists are performing a heroic, noble function on our behalf. That these are not people that are seeking the grandiosity. I mean, Danny never had any grandiosity about him. He never was looking for the bright lights.”

Along with Pearl’s charm and cultural sensitivity, Sonnenfeld said, the Wall Street Journal reporter had a refreshing sense of humility.

“He was exceptionally humble,” he said. “Merit kept putting him in spotlights, until tragedy put him in the spotlight.”
