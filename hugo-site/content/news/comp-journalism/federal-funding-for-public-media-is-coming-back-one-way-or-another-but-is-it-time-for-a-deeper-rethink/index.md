It was
[318 days ago](https://web.archive.org/web/20260105235905/https://cpb.org/pressroom/corporation-public-broadcasting-board-votes-dissolve-organization-act-responsible)
that the Corporation for Public Broadcasting — the primary funder of public media in the United States since 1967 —
[officially ceased to be](https://apnews.com/article/public-broadcasting-pbs-npr-b68f441c227ec7e076c038821b4a5931)
, the victim of Republican budget cuts and years of attacks from Donald Trump. CPB is not “on pause” or “in hibernation” — it’s dead. As its final CEO Patricia Harrison put it: “CPB’s final act would be to protect the integrity of the public media system and democratic values by dissolving, rather than allowing the organization to remain defunded and vulnerable to additional attacks.”

You may have heard, however, that
[we had some elections](https://www.nytimes.com/news-event/2026-midterm-elections)
in the U.S. recently, and the Republicans who defunded NPR and PBS will no longer control the House of Representatives or Senate as of January 3. In their place will be Congressional Democrats much more favorable to public media. So it seems quite likely that the federal government’s next budget will re-fund it rather than de-fund it.

But with CPB officially no more, Democrats will have a rare opportunity to rethink the system from scratch instead of just prescribing minor tweaks to funding levels. The media environment of 2027 will, it goes without saying, be radically different from 1967’s. Over-the-air broadcasting is no longer America’s dominant medium. The immensely profitable newspapers that were the bedrock of news back then are shells of their former selves. And what was once a highly localized information ecosystem has been thoroughly nationalized and globalized, with access to news increasingly determined by a few large tech platforms. Democrats
*could*
just revive the old LBJ-era model — but should they instead build something new?

These questions will dominate the next year of discourse around public media, and interested parties are already laying out plans. This morning, the Roosevelt Institute
[released a new white paper](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/creating-a-new-us-public-media-system/)
titled “Creating a New U.S. Public Media System: Normative Arguments and Policy Options.” Written by
[Penn professor](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/people/faculty/victor-pickard-phd)
(and
[past Nieman Lab contributor](https://www.niemanlab.org/author/vpickard/)
)
[Victor Pickard](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/authors/victor-pickard/)
, it argues for a thorough reinvention:

&gt; Taken together, the 2025 federal defunding of American public media, the ongoing collapse of local journalism, growing media oligarchy, a multitude of social harms arising from unregulated platforms and AI technologies, and other political and economic challenges to our media and democracy demonstrate the need for robust public media systems. Fundamental flaws — especially its funding mechanisms — are baked into the historical U.S. public media model, and they must be structurally addressed. The current crisis presents a unique opportunity to create an entirely new kind of public media system based on a local “Public Media Center” model.

Pickard lays out three core ideas: de-privatization, democratization, and localization.

**De-privatization**
means more thoroughly removing “public” media from the interests of the private sector. No more sponsorship messages from Monsanto or ExxonMobil, no more need to pitch corporations or beg listeners for the funds needed to pay reporters.

Pickard wants the public media system to be entirely publicly funded, to the tune of roughly $30 billion a year. That would be a massive increase — almost 60×! — from the pre-Trump level of federal funding, which was
[around $500 million a year](https://current.org/2022/03/biden-administration-requests-565m-for-cpb-in-fy25-supports-proposed-change-to-interconnection-funding/)
. Even if you include all non-federal sources of revenue, $30 billion would still represent a public media system 8 to 10 times larger than what exists today. (Pickard dreams big!)

But the fact that $30 billion sounds

*enormous*
is mostly about how small U.S. public media has always been compared to its international peers. Before defunding, the U.S. spent roughly $3 per person per year on public media.
[Compare that to countries](https://www.niemanlab.org/2022/01/do-countries-with-better-funded-public-media-also-have-healthier-democracies-of-course-they-do/)
like Norway ($110), Finland ($101), Denmark ($93), the U.K. ($81), France ($76), Czechia ($60), Japan ($53), Australia ($36), and Canada ($27). If Pickard got his $30 billion wish, U.S. public media funding would sit at about $88 per capita — BBC territory, roughly speaking. But even getting to the level of Canada or Australia would represent a huge increase.

By
**democratization**
, Pickard means confronting criticism that public media systems’ output often skews toward the interests of elites — either the incumbent government
[in a case like Viktor Orbán’s Hungary](https://www.ap.org/news-highlights/spotlights/2024/how-hungarys-orban-uses-control-of-the-media-to-escape-scrutiny-and-keep-the-public-in-the-dark/)
or the sort of “latte-sipping NPR liberals” that
[conservatives have inveighed against](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2025/oct/6/npr-loses-sugar-daddy-cpb/#:~:text=Sipping%20their%20%249%20lattes%20and%20putting%20middle%20America%20under%20their%20noses)
for decades:

&gt; Endeavoring to democratize public media institutions, therefore, is not only ethically sound but also strategic: Democratization can help ensure bipartisan trust, structural resilience, and journalistic independence. Toward this aim, a public media system should not be public in name only but actually owned and controlled by local communities. To encourage trust and community investment, this collective ownership and governance must translate to public participation in all stages of media production and dissemination. In fact, public media institutions are often cross-ideological islands of trust in an otherwise dismal landscape.
&gt;
&gt; This trust in public media can be further deepened through direct public ownership and participation, perhaps through community action programs,
&gt;
&gt; participatory budgeting, or some similar bottom-up model of democratic governance. Local media bureaus — rotating oversight boards that are locally elected or randomly selected — could help ensure additional public accountability.

And for Pickard,
**localization**
is a logical response to the de-localization of media that the internet hath wrought. Many public radio and TV stations primarily serve as distributors of national content, in large part because funding is nowhere near robust enough to create enough local programming to fill each day. And no matter how much you like Ken Burns or All Things Considered, sending the same programming nationwide can limit the range of a community’s residents who might be interested. He advocates pushing more control away from a federal-level entity like CPB and toward states, counties, and cities via block grants.

Pickard describes the nodes of this system as Public Media Centers: “multimedia hubs that are federally guaranteed but locally owned and democratically governed,” which “would provide journalism, broadcasting, digital media, and other information services based on community needs.”

Some might be housed in existing public radio or TV stations, others in post offices or libraries. And individual communities would be able to determine which media formats work best for them, rather than being locked into the expensive broadcast infrastructure required to reach audiences in 1967.

I am obliged to note that there are a
*huge*
number of barriers to Pickard’s vision becoming reality. Most obviously: $30 billion a year is a lot of money. That’s around the annual budget of the federal school lunch program ($32 billion), FEMA ($32 billion), or NASA ($25 billion). Secondly, the commercial media industry would not sit quietly while the federal government funds an enormous new rival that doesn’t need to sell ads or subscriptions. (For context, the entire U.S. newspaper industry pulls in
[around $20 billion a year](https://www.statista.com/statistics/242659/revenue-of-the-us-newspaper-publishing-industry/)
in revenue.) And American suspicion of “state-funded media” goes deep enough that I can’t imagine Democrats looking to retake the White House in 2028 deciding this is the hill to die on.

But the new Congress is still weeks away from convening, and the next federal fiscal year starts more than 10 months from now. This is the season for big dreams, for expanding the
[edges of the Overton window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overton_window)
, and in that sense, Pickard’s ideas should give newly empowered policymakers a lot to think about.
