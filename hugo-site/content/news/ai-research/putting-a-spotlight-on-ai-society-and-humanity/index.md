Supported by the MIT Human Insight Collaborative, scholars are exploring how AI is impacting the human experience and reshaping higher education.

MIT students are going to office hours less often. They’re spending less time working through problem sets, and with one another. Those were among the findings of MIT’s recent report on
[artificial intelligence and education](https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/)
, and they are one reason a growing number of MIT faculty are concerned about how generative AI is both changing how people work and affecting how they think, learn, and relate to one another.

For the
[MIT Human Insight Collaborative](https://mithic.mit.edu)
(MITHIC), those questions have become a central focus, and MITHIC is working with collaborators across the Institute, including the
[Social and Ethical Responsibilities of Computing](https://computing.mit.edu/cross-cutting/social-and-ethical-responsibilities-of-computing/)
(SERC), to help MIT address them.

Launched in 2024 as a strategic initiative, MITHIC brings together scholars, artists, educators, and researchers to explore the human dimensions of the defining challenges of our time. Since its launch, MITHIC has supported
[67 projects across 31 MIT units](https://mithic.mit.edu/impact/)
, offering a total of $5.5 million in funding.

Roughly a third of those projects are focused on AI, society, and humanity — a topic that MITHIC faculty co-lead
[Heather Paxson](https://anthropology.mit.edu/people/faculty/heather-paxson)
says has become an increasingly important part of the initiative.

“When we refer to AI and society, we’re not only speaking about individual ethical decision-making or how to retrain workers. We’re looking at how AI is reshaping sociality, how we
[relate](https://news.mit.edu/2026/when-we-talk-to-machines-sherry-turkle-book-0929)
to one another,” says Paxson, a professor of anthropology and the associate dean of the School of Humanities, Arts, and Social Sciences (SHASS).

**Eroding “creative friction”**

Building upon this focus, an
[AI and Society Forum](https://news.mit.edu/2026/exploring-societal-impacts-of-ai-0623)
took place on campus earlier this year. Co-organized by MITHIC, SHASS, SERC, and the
[MIT Generative AI Impact Consortium](https://genai.mit.edu/)
(MGAIC), the event featured presentations and panels concentrating on jobs, democracy, and AI.

On campus, the report on AI and education warned that AI is eroding the “creative friction” that has long defined an MIT education: students debating ideas and challenging one another, their professors, and their teaching assistants, in class and out of it.

MITHIC faculty co-lead
[Seth Mnookin](https://cmsw.mit.edu/profile/seth-mnookin/)
, a science writing professor and head of Comparative Media Studies Program/Writing (CMS/W), said, “The report wasn’t saying that AI is here and we all need to adapt. It was saying that we need to be asking more fundamental questions.”

“When the United States entered World War II in 1941, the Institute’s newly minted scientists and engineers helped shape that era through science and technology,” Mnookin said. “We’re at a similarly consequential moment with AI. The difference is that this time, the questions that matter most can’t be answered by technologists alone. That’s where the humanities, arts, and social sciences come in.”

**Crossing disciplines in the pursuit of answers**

MITHIC is supporting a range of interdisciplinary projects through its
[SHASS+ Connectivity Fund](https://mithic.mit.edu/shass-connectivity-fund/)
, in which a SHASS scholar partners with a researcher in another part of MIT.

In the context of the 2026 U.S. midterm elections, political science professors
[Adam Berinsky](https://polisci.mit.edu/people/adam-berinsky)
and
[Charles Stewart III](https://polisci.mit.edu/people/charles-stewart-iii)
and MIT Sloan School of Management Assistant Professor
[Chara Podimata](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/faculty/directory/chara-podimata)
are studying how large language models (LLMs) have become intermediaries of democratic knowledge.

The ongoing project, which has demonstrated how voters in Alaska’s midterms who conducted their pre-election research using Anthropic’s AI model, Claude, may have received incomplete information about candidates and their views, was recently
[featured in
*The New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/10/business/media/ai-chatbots-election-misinformation.html?unlocked_article_code=1.IVE.8Kr5.eEpKyyfrOLzA&amp;smid=url-share)
.

“AI is becoming part of the way people encounter and make sense of political information, and yet we know relatively little about what that information environment actually looks like, how it differs for different user demographics, political identities, and geographies,” Podimata told the
*Times*
.

In another project, CMS/W Professor
[Eric Klopfer](https://cmsw.mit.edu/profile/eric-klopfer/)

and brain and cognitive sciences Professor
[Pawan Sinha](https://bcs.mit.edu/directory/pawan-sinha)
are co-leading research that’s measuring AI’s near- and long-term effects on cognition.

Meeting at the intersection of AI, education, and neuroscience, the professors are examining whether generative AI is increasing the human brain’s consumption of information but offloading cognitive function, or supporting an increase in cognitive function when used as part of the creative process. They recently gathered experts on the topic for an event on the MIT campus, “
[AI and the Brain](https://mithic.mit.edu/how-does-ai-impact-your-brain/)
.”

In the field of arts and technology,
[Anna Huang](https://www.eecs.mit.edu/people/cheng-zhi-anna-huang/)
, a professor of music technology holding an MIT Schwarzman College of Computing shared position with the Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science,
[Joe Paradiso](https://www.media.mit.edu/people/joep/overview/)
, a professor at the MIT Media Lab, are examining how incorporating AI impacts the creative process and live performance experience.

Their project, “Live Musician-AI Improvisation, Tuning the Social Aesthetics in Human-AI Partnerships” explores the question: what happens when human composers and contemporary machine learning systems improvise to make music together?

As part of their project, a collective of MIT musicians, ethnomusicologists, and engineers meets for regular sessions to experiment with improvisation frameworks like stopping to discuss how technology impacts authorship and musical direction, ensemble dynamics, and other cornerstones of improvisation. Faculty will ultimately incorporate their takeaways into a future course or practicum on AI-improvisation.

**Teaching and scholarship in an AI era**

SHASS faculty and academic staff are also leading new MITHIC initiatives to help broaden instruction and community dialogue about the intersection of AI and society.

[Graham Jones](https://anthropology.mit.edu/people/faculty/graham-jones)
, professor of anthropology, and
[Arvind Satyanarayan](https://www.eecs.mit.edu/people/arvind-satyanarayan/)
, associate professor of computer science, used MITHIC’s SHASS+ Connectivity funding to help launch a course,
[6.S061/21A.S02 (Humane User Experience Design)](https://anthropology.mit.edu/21A.S02_21A.S10_Humane_User_Experience_Design)
, in which students examine how AI systems are designed and grapple with the consequences of AI agents entering into socio-cultural relationships with people through their ability to mimic human thought and speech.

“The crux of our collaboration is the term 'humane.' It’s a bit of a double-entendre, meaning both human-serving or benevolent, and humanlike,” Jones said at the
[inaugural MITHIC Annual Event](https://youtu.be/GOToub5Mm8Q?si=mynDMTCtP3-sxlcH)
. “What’s interesting is that our students are discovering that there’s a fundamental tension between building a system that’s good for people and one that seems human, because real people can develop dangerous dependencies.”

Another project, “Rebuilding Computing and Society at MIT,” is supported by MITHIC’s Education Impact Fund (EIF). Led by
[Dwai Banerjee](https://sts-program.mit.edu/people/sts-faculty/dwai-banerjee/)
, associate professor of science, technology, and society, the initiative revitalized the
[Computing and Society concentration](https://shass.mit.edu/for-students/undergraduate-study/computing-and-society-concentration/)
launched in 2019. The HASS concentration had gone dormant, listing classes no longer being offered and with minimal student interest. The concentration now features current classes across 11 SHASS units in which students can explore computing through different humanistic and social science angles.

Banerjee, who serves as the concentration’s advisor, is also working with lecturer and research affiliate
[Mariel Garcia-Montes PhD ’26](https://cmsw.mit.edu/profile/mariel-garcia-montes/)
to find ways to connect the concentration instructors and campus initiatives on computing and society. MIT senior Daniel Gonzalez is also part of the project, developing a research and outreach program that centers undergraduate voices in computing and society efforts across the Institute.

Another MITHIC project funded by the EIF, “Review of Generative AI Assistive and Resistant Communication Assignments and Supporting Workshops,” is led by
[Michael Trice](https://cmsw.mit.edu/profile/michael-trice/)
, a lecturer in MIT’s
[Writing, Rhetoric, and Professional Communication (WRAP) program.](https://cmsw.mit.edu/academics/writing-rhetoric-professional-communication/)

The project is responding to a consistent SHASS faculty request for additional training and skills needed “to fully address generative AI concerns in their classes.”

Trice is creating a bank of faculty resources and facilitating workshops and visiting lectures on generative AI and writing. These include a
[talk](https://youtu.be/CV-S6_jGauc?si=fH9OrRfXQi_JV4Ag)
given earlier this fall by John Gallagher from the University of Illinois, who presented findings from a large-scale survey on AI-usage and writing conducted with undergraduates at his institution in 2026.

**Meeting the moment**

MIT leaders agree that MITHIC, now in its third year, is playing a central role in the Institute meeting the moment, as AI presents both extraordinary potential and disturbing risks.

“There has never been a more important time for constructive collaboration between science and technology and the human-centered disciplines,” MIT President Sally Kornbluth recently said. “Through MITHIC, MIT is giving faculty the support to reach out across disciplines and pursue their dreams.”

Agustín Rayo, dean of SHASS and co-chair of MITHIC, agrees. “MIT is uniquely placed to help us understand this extraordinary moment and to generate the values, tools, and policies that we will need to shape AI so that it becomes a force for good,” he said. “It is essential that we deploy AI technology in a way that elevates human existence, rather than diminishing it.”

At the recent SHASS 75th Anniversary Symposium, Provost Anantha Chandrakasan said given the rapid development of AI, an understanding of the ethical implications and societal impact of implementing new technologies is especially urgent.

“SHASS provides an essential perspective on questions concerning how technology can be developed thoughtfully and deployed responsibly, with the aim of improving human well-being,” said Chandrakasan, who also serves as MITHIC co-chair.

Looking ahead, MITHIC and SERC are developing a series of events that pair humanists or social scientists with colleagues in a single field, starting with mathematics, to examine what AI is changing about how that field produces knowledge and trains its next generation.

“The question isn’t whether AI will change these fields,” Mnookin said. “It’s whether we understand what’s at stake before it does.”
