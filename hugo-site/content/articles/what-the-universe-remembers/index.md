You may not remember the decision that changed your life.

Perhaps it was an ordinary Tuesday when you accepted a job in another city. Perhaps you answered a message you nearly ignored, or decided, without much ceremony, that you would stop drinking, learn a language, or have a child. The feeling of choosing lasted seconds. Years later, the consequences have become your address, your friends, your reflexes, and the particular people who exist because your life crossed theirs.

The choice is gone as an experience. It is not gone as a cause.

In [*Where Choice Enters the World*](https://gtcode.com/articles/where-choice-enters-the-world/), published October 10, we introduced [Conditional Ontological Agency Theory (COAT)](/articles/where-choice-enters-the-world/#the-formal-core-of-coat) to separate several ideas usually forced into the same argument: genuine authorship at a moment, the effects of an earlier choice, ordinary biological control, and the more radical possibility of choosing which life is experienced.[^1] That essay asked whether consciousness can ever change what happens next.

A recent physics story suggests a different question. What if the most interesting feature of a choice is not its moment of occurrence, but the way reality carries its history forward?

If conscious authorship exists, perhaps it begins in preparation: an author helps settle the conditions from which a history unfolds. Quantum birthmarks sharpen the question that follows. What does that history retain, and who could read it?

## A quantum system with a past

On October 11, 2026, Ingrid Fadelli reported for *Phys.org* on **quantum birthmarks**.[^2] Anton M. Graf and colleagues' study appeared in *Physical Review X* on September 10; its first preprint dates to December 2024.[^3][^18] The recent coverage follows nearly two years of work in public.

Imagine a particle confined inside a stadium-shaped enclosure: a rectangle with a semicircle at either end. A classical ball follows a deterministic path, yet its long-time location statistics can conceal where it began. The quantum counterpart retains a statistical preference for its initial state and every state into which that state evolves. Here, *matched* comparison states have the same mean energy and energy spread.[^3]

For generic states in the companion paper's random-matrix systems, the universal enhancement approaches two without time-reversal symmetry and three with it as the effective dimension grows. Properly matching energy and symmetry constraints restores those baselines; comparisons against the full, unreduced Hilbert space can give larger factors. Early recurrences supply a further enhancement.[^19] Quantum scars, associated with unstable periodic orbits since Heller's 1984 paper, are one especially strong case.[^20]

In the stadium simulations, generic launches produced an enhancement of about two; launches along periodic orbits reached about eight.[^4]

The persistent mark appears in an infinite-time average under isolated, unitary evolution. Richter states the condition plainly: the birthmark should persist while quantum coherence is maintained, and fading would signal decoherence.[^4] A biological analogue remains a metaphor until someone identifies a mechanism and a way to read it. The argument about authorship and experience that follows is philosophical conjecture. The birthmark study supplies the physics of the trace, not evidence of a chooser.

## Memory without a rememberer

For a time-independent Hamiltonian with nondegenerate energy levels, write the initial state as $|\psi(0)\rangle=\sum_n c_n|E_n\rangle$. Its infinite-time-averaged return probability is

$$
\overline{P}_{\mathrm{return}}=\sum_n |c_n|^4.
$$

This is the inverse participation ratio in the energy basis, also called the dilution factor in the paper. A large value means the initial state is concentrated in few energy eigenstates; a smaller value means it is spread across more of them. The birthmark claim concerns its enhancement relative to matched states, with a further contribution from early revivals.[^3]

Three kinds of memory need separating:

| Kind of memory | What persists | What persistence leaves unanswered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Informational | Unitary evolution preserves distinctions between initial states. | Can a later observer recover them? |
| Statistical | A birthmark survives the long-time average in matched comparisons. | Which observable exposes it? |
| Accessible | A physical record can affect another system, as environmental records do in quantum Darwinism. | Does any subject experience that record? |

The result matters at the second level. Preserving information under unitarity was already expected. A surviving bias in statistics that seem to have equilibrated is the sharper surprise. The paper shows that averaging over finer spatial structure can smooth the visible pattern while the underlying enhancement survives.[^3] Richter discusses its apparent tension with eigenstate thermalization and emphasizes the role of the initial state's spectral decomposition.[^4]

A surviving trace needs a reader before it can influence behavior. Quantum Darwinism describes how interactions distribute records into the environment, where other systems can encounter them.[^5] Experienced access raises a further question: why should that encounter feel like something?

The open-system question is already an active research program. In a March 2026 APS meeting abstract, Graf, Keski-Rahkonen, and Heller described extending birthmarks to open systems and using them to probe decoherence channels.[^21] The abstract reports a theoretical extension, rather than an experimental demonstration. It asks a concrete question: can the weakening of a birthmark reveal how a system loses coherence?

The paper relates the characteristic timescale to the Heisenberg time, set by the inverse mean energy-level spacing.[^3] In typical many-body systems, the number of available states grows exponentially with system size, the spacing shrinks, and this time grows exponentially too.[^33] That is a serious obstacle to reading a long-time statistical signature within a biological timescale. It does not make every short-time trace inaccessible. Richter identifies the strength of scar enhancement in many-body systems as an open question.[^4]

## The ancestor you have already become

The person you were ten years ago has more power over your present than many people standing beside you. That earlier person chose where to live, whom to trust, what to learn, which fears to confront, and which habits to rehearse. Their decisions now arrive as circumstances and dispositions. Your past self has become, in a literal causal sense, one of your ancestors.

Picture two lives that differ at one fork. In one, you make the difficult apology; in the other, you keep silent. The next morning the differences may be tiny. A decade later they could include different relationships, work, children, and convictions. Learning, memory, and habit carry those consequences through ordinary causation.

Robert Kane's *self-forming actions* address the stronger possibility: some difficult, undetermined choices help form the character from which later actions flow.[^6] COAT calls that continuity *inherited authorship*. The question is whether any act in the chain was yours in a stronger sense than functional control.

Extend the apology across generations. A reconciliation can shape a family, a migration, or whether particular people exist. Genes, bodies, institutions, and stories carry the history. If the original act was authored, those mechanisms could carry its consequences long after its author died.

> Authentic authorship might be intermittent. One authored choice could shape years of ordinary causation through the habits and commitments it establishes.

One moment could be yours in a strong sense. Much of the life that follows could be what that moment becomes.

## The witness, the author, and the traveler

Consider a person who pauses at a doorway and walks back inside to help a stranger. Their brain prepares movement, muscles respond, and the consequences spread. What would make this a free act?

**Functional agency** is the person's ability to weigh reasons, inhibit an impulse, and act on a commitment. Compatibilist accounts can recognize this control within a fully physical causal history. **Authorship** asks whether the subject also helps settle an alternative that its unchosen past has left open. Randomness alone cannot supply that role.[^7]

**Experiential selection** asks a different question: could a subject occupy one admissible life rather than another? We use that term for the traveler's relation to a history. COAT's broader *existential selection* also includes the stronger proposal that selection determines physical boundary conditions. The earlier essay made this distinction in its [existential-selection section](/articles/where-choice-enters-the-world/#existential-selection).

COAT separates three ontologies and four coordinates. $\mathcal O_0$ contains physical processes only; $\mathcal O_1$ adds a noncausal experiencing subject; $\mathcal O_2$ adds a causally active subject. $E$ denotes existential selection, $I_t$ local intervention, $L_t$ inherited authorship, and $G_t$ functional agency.[^1]

| Picture | Ontology | $E$ | $I_t$ | $L_t$ | $G_t$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Embodied control | $\mathcal O_0$ | 0 | 0 | 0 | Can be strong |
| Witness | $\mathcal O_1$ | 0 | 0 | 0 | Can be strong |
| Local intermittent author | $\mathcal O_2$ | Not required | Sometimes nonzero: changes $K_0$ | Can persist after intervention | Can be strong |
| Global boundary author | $\mathcal O_2$ as global determination | 1, boundary selection | 0 within the selected history | 0 under the local-intervention definition | Can be strong |
| Traveler | $\mathcal O_1$ with a selection premise | 1 | 0 | 0 from local intervention | Can be strong |

Here $I_t$ retains its earlier definition: a local intervention changes the physical transition kernel from $K_0$ to $K_\theta$. Global boundary selection is a different object, represented here by $E$. Likewise, $L_t$ measures inheritance from local intervention, so it can be zero even when a globally selected history contains a lifetime of consequences. Calling those consequences inherited authorship uses the ordinary phrase more broadly than the formal coordinate. The traveler follows the earlier essay's convention that $E=1$ can coexist with a life without local intervention. Its selection premise adds something to $\mathcal O_1$: a noncausal subject alone need not choose a life.

The witness accompanies a history. The author helps determine it. The traveler experiences one among the histories available. Each can coexist with ordinary functional agency, but they assign different roles to the subject.

## The boundary-condition wager

**If authorship exists beyond ordinary functional control, this essay wagers on the global preparation of a history.** The earlier essay suggested that existential selection could determine boundary conditions. The wager develops that suggestion: authorship supplies an input, and physical law governs what follows.

The earlier essay's [quantum-intervention model](/articles/where-choice-enters-the-world/#hypothetical-quantum-probability-biasing) allowed a subject to tilt outcome probabilities. Such a model must explain how its changes preserve entanglement constraints and no-signalling. Selection of a global boundary condition can preserve subsequent dynamics and the Born rule. Physics already describes what follows from different preparations; the disputed addition is a subject's power to settle one.

Choose the seed of a deterministic simulation. The same program produces a different history without changing its rules. The seed carries information about which history unfolds. Someone inside that simulation cannot reset its seed simply by calling an internal event a fresh input. The analogy works only if we keep the whole simulation in view.

### Local means closure; global means cosmic

Return to the apology. In the local version, the subject settles a preparation that its complete prior physical state did not settle. Under complete physical closure, however, that preparation is already governed by $K_0$. An additional subject-level variable with no effect on the kernel has no independent modeled efficacy, as the earlier essay's [first conditional proposition](/articles/where-choice-enters-the-world/#conditional-propositions) states. To change what happens, the local author has to change the transition account. Lewis's small counterfactual miracle supplies an analogy for such a departure from the original laws.[^30] The closure cost of probability biasing has moved to preparation, not disappeared.

In the global version, the apology belongs to a whole admissible history. Its boundary conditions also constrain the other person, the room, and the world that brought them together. A life is no isolated seed. Global selection therefore has cosmic scope, even if the proposed subject's concern is one conversation. It need not involve a first instant: a constraint on a complete history can be global without occurring before it.

**The wager takes the global horn.** An apology then follows the laws within the history; the proposed authorship concerns the whole history containing it. We are asking whether a subject can own that history, not whether it can add a cause within complete physical closure. The conversation at the door has become a claim about a world. Fine-tuning and simulation hypotheses face a related demand: specify the alternatives, the selection rule, and why intentional selection explains more than impersonal constraints.

Bell's theorem makes the status of inputs especially clear. Measurement independence requires the measurement settings to be statistically independent of the hidden variables used to describe the measured pair. That mathematical assumption is distinct from a claim that experimenters possess ultimate authorship. Superdeterminist models relax it; Conway and Kochen's theorem likewise draws a conditional conclusion from a premise about experimenter freedom.[^22][^23] Global boundary selection therefore does not settle the freedom-of-choice problem. Even a history that obeys its laws can correlate measurement settings with hidden variables. A concrete model has to state whether it preserves measurement independence or relaxes it.

Retrocausal models offer one physical setting in which to examine the wager. Wharton and Argaman review models solved partly all at once, with intermediate variables depending on future measurement settings.[^22] Price's treatment of time encourages the same shift in viewpoint.[^24] In such a formulation, boundary conditions constrain a whole history. The proposed choice would have to fit those constraints throughout the history, including the circumstances that brought the two people to the door.

A broken cup records a past that its scattered pieces rarely reverse. Albert's Past Hypothesis helps explain such thermodynamic asymmetry by positing a special low-entropy past.[^25] It shows how much explanatory work a boundary condition can do. The Past Hypothesis specifies a macrocondition, however, and neither selects a unique life nor supplies an author. Richter also suggests that birthmarks may inform research on the emergence of an arrow of time.[^4] The common question is how equilibration relates to the direction of time. A low-entropy boundary and a quantum birthmark play different roles in that question; neither supplies an author.

### Who authors the author?

Galen Strawson's Basic Argument presses the deepest objection. We choose through our character and preferences. Ultimate responsibility for the choice would seem to require responsibility for the character that chooses; an earlier act of self-formation would itself require a prior chooser. Determinism and randomness both confront this regress.[^26]

Kane replies through self-forming actions: a person can help shape their character through divided efforts at an undetermined choice point, without having created themselves first.[^6] A luck objection remains: if the same prior efforts can end in apology or silence, what makes the eventual outcome the agent's responsibility rather than good or bad fortune? Kane argues that purposive effort can sustain responsibility; the objection challenges whether it supplies the ultimate ownership Strawson demands.[^7]

A block or two-boundary formulation suggests a possible reply: perhaps authorship is a relation within the whole consistent history, rather than an event produced by an earlier, self-created chooser. On that proposal, a subject's reasons and the selected boundary belong to one global constraint. Asking for a temporally prior act of self-creation would impose a sequence the model does not contain.

The global reply avoids requiring indeterminism at the choice point; the apology can follow deterministically within the selected history. Its cost is *ownership*: why does a consistent history count as something the subject authored? Even without a prior moment, Strawson can still ask why the subject owns the reasons that constrain the history. A successful version of the wager would have to explain ownership through the relation between subject, reasons, and history, rather than simply naming a global solution a choice.

**At the global limit, the author and traveler differ over the status of the other histories.** If selection makes only one concrete history exist, the subject is an author in this sense. If every history already exists and selection concerns which one is experienced, the subject is a traveler. Hold the account of subject and selection fixed, and the dispute turns toward modal metaphysics: what exists, and what makes a world actual? Lewis's indexical account treats actuality as relative to the world of the speaker, rather than a status conferred on one world.[^31] It supplies no chooser who moves between worlds. The wager now owes an account of actuality as well as ownership.

## Does the quantum world ever matter to an ordinary mind?

Before you speak the apology, your warm, chemically active brain prepares it. The local version of the wager faces both this biological mechanism and the closure problem; biology alone cannot resolve the latter. The earlier essay cites both [Tegmark's decoherence estimates](/articles/where-choice-enters-the-world/#fn:31) and [Hagan, Hameroff, and Tuszyński's response](/articles/where-choice-enters-the-world/#fn:32). Their disagreement concerns specific proposed quantum mechanisms and timescales. A birthmark model would need its own calculation.

Rod photoreceptors can respond to individual photons, as Rieke and Baylor review.[^32] Whether a person consciously detects a photon is a further question. Tinsley and colleagues reported 51.6% correct responses in a single-photon detection task with three participants; the overall experiment contained 30,767 trials, of which 2,420 passed single-photon post-selection. A subsequent correspondence disputed whether the evidence supports conscious single-photon detection.[^13] A separate experiment found magnetic sensitivity in European robin cryptochrome 4 in vitro, showing how a quantum-sensitive chemical process could contribute to biology.[^14]

A quantum-sensitive event could change a chemical yield, alter a cell, and affect a neural network. Once the change becomes a durable record, ordinary causation can carry it forward. A birthmark account must identify the process that retains the relevant statistical bias, the molecule or apparatus that reads it, and the timescale on which it affects behavior. The open-system research described above could help define those requirements.

This route also separates physical influence from authorship. A fluctuation that tips a neural network supplies variation. The author's proposed contribution is to settle a preparation for reasons. Penrose and Hameroff's Orch OR theory assigns quantum processes a different role in consciousness and would require a separate argument.[^15] These mechanisms could explain how a small difference reaches behavior. The global wager instead concerns why a whole history, including its neural mechanisms, is the one authored or experienced.

## The hard problem survives every causal history

Suppose tomorrow we learn the complete causal history of one person's thoughts. We can trace every neural spike, every hormone signal, every inherited disposition, and every microscopic event that helped determine the result. We can even identify the earlier decisions whose consequences still shape the person.

One question remains: why should those processes feel like anything from the inside?

David Chalmers called this the *hard problem of consciousness*. Explaining the functions associated with consciousness—reporting, attention, memory, discrimination—does not automatically explain the existence of subjective experience.[^8]

Theories such as global neuronal workspace theory describe how information can become widely available to neural systems. Integrated information theory asks whether a system's intrinsic causal structure relates to the character and unity of experience. A major 2025 adversarial collaboration tested predictions from these theories, finding results that challenged central expectations of both while supporting some components.[^9][^10]

Those programs address important questions. Yet even a perfect story about neural information and physical memory could leave a philosophical gap between *what the system does* and *why there is someone for whom it happens*.

The distinction between accessible and experienced memory puts that gap in focus. A theory of records explains how information reaches a system. A theory of consciousness must explain the experience of encountering it. Whether that experience also authors anything is a further question.

## Which views of consciousness can host an author?

The person hearing an apology experiences more than its acoustic pattern. A physical account can trace the signal through their nervous system. Views that make experience fundamental ask what that account describes from the inside, and whether subjectivity also helps explain why this history occurs.

Three proposals bring different strengths and difficulties to the wager:

| View | What it contributes | What the wager still asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Panpsychism | Experiential or proto-experiential properties are basic. | How do elementary contributions constitute one subject capable of owning a history? |
| Russellian monism | Experience may belong to the intrinsic nature underlying physical structure and causation. | Does participating in physical causation supply authorship, or only functional control? |
| Cosmopsychism | Individual minds may be perspectives of a cosmic subject. | Can one subject account for private perspectives, and can it select among them? |

Panpsychism's combination problem bears directly on the author: many experiential ingredients do not yet specify the person who apologizes.[^11] Cosmopsychism offers a conceptual home for the traveler, but explaining how one cosmic subject has bounded perspectives is already difficult. Selecting those perspectives adds another task.[^11]

Many Russellian monists aim to integrate experience into physical causation.[^12] An embodied subject could therefore be causally active through physics without changing a transition kernel. That fits functional agency, but it does not by itself provide COAT's independent local intervention or this essay's global selection. The witness row in the earlier table describes $\mathcal O_1$ specifically; it is not a classification of Russellian monism as a whole.

## Could the first choice have been a life?

Plato's Myth of Er imagines souls choosing lives before embodiment and forgetting the choice.[^16] Closer philosophical relatives of the wager appear in Kant's distinction between empirical and intelligible character and Schopenhauer's account of freedom in what one *is* rather than each action one performs: *esse* rather than *operari*.[^27] These ideas relocate freedom, though they offer different accounts of its relation to a life.

The traveler's question is sharper. Imagine a physically determined life. Could a subject still have freedom over whether that is the life it experiences?

Self-locating belief distinguishes a complete world-description from knowing one's position within it. A map and its *you are here* mark convey different information. A **centered world** represents a world, time, and individual without assuming a soul.[^17] Experiential selection adds the proposal that a subject could choose a center. From within the selected life, events could retain their ordinary physical causes.

Albert and Loewer's many-minds interpretation is a relevant precedent because it separates mental histories from a unitarily evolving quantum state.[^28] It does not grant a person the power to choose a preferred branch. That further power is precisely what the traveler hypothesis would need to explain.

Who is the subject before selection? How can it prefer a life it has never lived? If it remembers nothing of the selection, what makes it the same subject? Parfit's reductionist treatment of identity supplies a direct challenge: perhaps psychological and physical continuity exhaust the facts about a person, leaving no additional bearer to assign to a life.[^29] If that reductionist account is right, experiential selection would need to explain what, beyond those continuities, does the selecting.

The traveler's prospects depend on this account of identity. If subjects are constituted by their embodied histories, experiential selection has no independent traveler. If subjectivity extends beyond those histories, the theory must explain its continuity and preferences. A fixed life leaves two questions to distinguish: what determines its events, and why a subject experiences them.

## What a history leaves behind

Return to the stadium. The quantum state retains information, its long-time statistics retain a birthmark, and a suitable measurement might reveal the trace. Those are three distinct claims. Whether any access is experienced belongs to a fourth question.

Return to the person at the beginning of this essay. They may have forgotten the conversation or decision. Their life carries it through relationships, learned tendencies, bodily habits, and events that would otherwise never have occurred. Their experience gives some of that history an inside.

The global wager asks whether a subject can own a whole history without creating itself in an earlier moment. It preserves physical law within that history and accepts cosmic scope in return. Whether the subject authors a world or travels through one then depends partly on whether the alternatives exist.

Suppose the Tuesday decision was authored. Authorship would connect you to the history containing that decision; inheritance would connect it to your address, habits, and friends. Physical access would explain how some of that history reaches you now. Experienced access would explain why remembering it feels like something. We have pulled those relations apart so that each can be questioned on its own.

We often look for freedom at the instant a decision is made. If a deeper freedom exists, its signature may reach beyond the feeling of control into the world a choice leaves behind.
