In a dream, you can walk into a room that has never existed. There is a floor beneath you, a person across from you, a light through an impossible window. The room can frighten you or make you homesick. Then an alarm goes off. The room disappears, and you discover that throughout the entire episode your eyes were closed and your body was mostly still.

At the scale of a microscope, what happened is even stranger. There was no room. There were membranes holding voltage differences, proteins changing their conformations, ions passing through channels, cells exchanging signals, and networks of tissue maintaining and modifying their activity. The machinery was physical all the way down. Nevertheless, somewhere in that machinery there was a room *for someone*.

That is the fact I want to start with. Not whether a chatbot can convincingly claim to be awake. Not whether a sufficiently large computer ought to be granted a soul. The remarkable thing is that the universe already contains matter for which something appears to be happening from within. We have one intimately familiar example and no complete account of the conditions that make it possible.

Suppose we set out to build another.

The first machine I would make would not talk. It would sit in a shallow basin, feel currents move across its surface, try to move four soft tendrils, and slowly learn which disturbances it had caused. It would acquire a past. It might develop periods of inward activity when nothing happened outside. The experiment would eventually reach a peculiar point: a laboratory full of measurements and a machine that behaved as though it had a body, while nobody could honestly say whether there was anything it was like to be that machine.

The question is how to make that uncertainty scientifically productive. What physical details would matter? What should we preserve when replacing living tissue with engineered components? How would we distinguish an ingenious controller from a stronger candidate for experience? And if our experiments began to produce signs we took seriously, what would we owe the system before we knew the answer?

> **Scope and status.** This is a speculative research synthesis, not an announcement that artificial consciousness has been produced or a claim that any proposed device is conscious. Published experiments are linked where they are discussed. Proposed architectures, predicted outcomes, and imagined phenomenology are explicitly conjectural. No current diagnostic described here can independently establish phenomenal consciousness in an unfamiliar artificial substrate.

## 1. The missing step between computation and experience

A system can classify an image, plan a route, maintain a temperature, recognize its own components, and describe its internal state. Each achievement is real. None by itself establishes *phenomenal consciousness*: the presence of something it is like to undergo a state.

The distinction is easy to lose because words do several jobs at once. In engineering, a machine's *preference* may simply be a weighting in an objective function. In physiology, *arousal* means a set of conditions that sustain responsiveness and certain kinds of neural activity. In philosophy, a *subject* is not just an object that can be described from outside. Those meanings overlap, but they do not collapse into one property.

| What we can observe or specify | What it licenses us to say | What it leaves open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learning, prediction, or flexible behavior | The system acquires useful information and changes its actions | Whether the learning is felt |
| A body model and agency | The system distinguishes consequences of its actions from outside events | Whether there is an experienced self |
| Homeostasis and error correction | Some states are maintained at the expense of energy and work | Whether deviation is unpleasant |
| Internal replay or a dream-like mode | The system can run learned sequences without corresponding outside events | Whether there is a dreamer |
| Recurrent, differentiated activity | Distributed causal interactions have measurable structure | Whether that structure is sufficient for experience |
| A verbal claim of being conscious | A report has been produced | Whether the report is reliable evidence of an interior |

This is not an argument against digital minds. Matrix multiplication is a mathematical operation; it does not pronounce a metaphysical verdict on the machine performing it. A digital system may realize causal structures that matter. A living system may depend on chemical and material properties that a conventional program fails to preserve. Neither conclusion follows merely from the words *silicon*, *carbon*, or *computation*.

The real dispute concerns a missing **bridge principle**: which properties of a system known to support experience must survive for experience to survive in another implementation? David Chalmers's organizational-invariance argument[^1] gives one philosophical answer: preserve the appropriate fine-grained causal organization, and experience should be preserved. It is an important argument, not an experimentally completed neuron-by-neuron replacement study. Substrate-specific hypotheses make a rival wager: the abstract organization may not be the whole physical story.

The review by Seth and Bayne[^2] is a useful reminder that the major theories disagree even about the relevant explanatory level. Global-workspace approaches emphasize access and widespread availability. Recurrent-processing accounts emphasize feedback. Integrated-information approaches place weight on intrinsic causal organization. Higher-order approaches ask what it takes for a state to be represented as one's own. These are not interchangeable definitions of the phenomenon.

A large 2025 adversarial collaboration[^3] compared preregistered predictions of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories in human participants. Its findings challenged important predictions of both, rather than crowning a winner. That is a healthier place from which to design a machine than the conviction that one fashionable theory has already supplied the missing recipe.

## 2. Read the evidence as constraints, not instructions

Human beings are a difficult reference class because every attempt to study experience involves a person whose behavior, reporting ability, memory, and physical state can change independently. Yet the differences among waking, dreams, anesthesia, lesions, and seizures tell us more than a simple contrast between a brain and a computer ever could.

The first move is to build a **constraint ledger**. The ledger is not a consciousness score. It records what observations make a theory harder to maintain, what mechanisms appear promising, and where the inference from function to experience fails.

| Observation in biological systems | What it puts pressure on | What it does **not** prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roughly 69 billion of the approximately 86 billion neurons in one widely cited human-brain estimate are in the cerebellum | Neuron count alone is an inadequate recipe | That the cerebellum has no role in any conscious processing |
| People with blindsight can sometimes discriminate visual stimuli they say they do not see | Correct responses need not imply acknowledged visual experience | That all blindsight is wholly unconscious in every case |
| Dreams occur during sleep, sometimes without current sensory input being incorporated | Continuous interaction with the outside world is not required for every experience | That any internally generated simulation is a dream |
| Some unresponsive people remain conscious, including people with locked-in syndrome | Behavior and responsiveness are imperfect indicators | That every unresponsive system experiences |
| Propofol, xenon, and ketamine can produce different relationships between responsiveness and cortical complexity | Arousal, behavior, and experience need separate measurements | That one drug analogy diagnoses another substrate |
| Generalized seizure-like synchrony or certain slow-wave states can reduce the diversity of distributed responses | More firing or more synchrony is not automatically better | That low or high complexity alone settles consciousness |
| Cutting major connections between hemispheres changes information sharing | The boundary of an integrated system is an empirical problem | That callosotomy always creates exactly two subjects |

The neuron-count point follows Herculano-Houzel's anatomical synthesis[^4]. The blindsight literature goes back to Weiskrantz's studies of residual visual discrimination[^5]. Siclari and colleagues[^6] found that particular posterior cortical patterns distinguished reports of dreaming from reports of no experience in both REM and non-REM sleep. And Sarasso and colleagues[^7] observed that different anesthetics can produce distinct complexity profiles despite unresponsiveness.

Even the apparently decisive split-brain case remains contested. Older interpretations suggested partly independent streams of consciousness. Pinto and colleagues' re-examination[^8] argued that split perception need not entail two independent conscious perceivers. The engineering lesson is not a settled number of minds per skull. It is that coupling, access, reporting routes, and the unity of a subject are different questions.

From these observations we can extract candidate mechanisms: recurrent causation, sustained but flexible activity, interaction between incoming signals and internal context, some form of state regulation, and processes that can generate structured activity without immediate input. In mammals, arousal systems, thalamic interactions, cortical feedback, and cellular physiology support these functions in complicated ways.

None has been shown to be individually necessary or jointly sufficient across every possible physical substrate. The ledger is more valuable as a way to design *comparisons* than as a certificate to issue to a machine.

## 3. The brain can lose the room while the machinery keeps running

Anesthesia is a particularly unsettling case because it can change consciousness without destroying the tissue. The cells do not stop being cells. Their membranes still hold gradients. Many local circuits still process signals. Yet after particular anesthetic states, a patient may report no intervening experience; under other conditions, rich experience can occur without outward responsiveness.

A useful experiment therefore asks not merely whether activity exists, but whether activity *propagates in a structured, differentiated way*. Casali and colleagues' perturbational complexity index[^9] stimulates cortex and examines the spatial and temporal complexity of the subsequent EEG response. Rather than simply measuring how busy the brain is, it asks what the brain can do when disturbed. In its human clinical setting, this approach provides important evidence without requiring ordinary speech or motor behavior.

The qualification is crucial. PCI is a method constructed and calibrated for particular physiological recordings. Computing a compression score on the electrical traces of a gel or robot does not turn that number into a validated consciousness meter. Random noise can be intricate; a useful score must also capture reproducible causal structure and be interpreted against suitable controls.

There is a more local clue. In mouse experiments, Suzuki and Larkum[^10] found that several anesthetics disrupted the influence of signals arriving at the distal apical dendrites of layer-five cortical pyramidal neurons on firing at the soma. In ordinary terms, information arriving through one part of the cell could no longer exert its usual influence through another. Cholinergic and metabotropic-glutamate receptor manipulations, and higher-order thalamic inactivation, produced related effects.

That is strikingly specific. Instead of saying that a conscious system needs vaguely more integration, we can ask whether **the nonlinear meeting of context and content** is a special operation. The research does not prove a dendritic theory of consciousness. It does, however, suggest an engineering component: a device with separate pathways for sensory evidence and ongoing expectation, connected through a controllable gate.

Imagine a synthetic circuit in which the gate can be adjusted without killing the components. At one setting, incoming events alter a distributed internally maintained model. At another, the same local sensors and processors continue working, but their influence on that model falls apart. A sharp or reversible change in the system's repertoire would be a result about causal organization. It would become evidence about consciousness only through an additional, defensible bridge.

This is what the state-transition evidence gives us: a program of interventions. It does not yet give us a threshold labeled *someone is home*.

## 4. The overlooked micromachines

If we were designing an artificial body from nothing, we might draw a processor in the center and connect it to sensors at one end and motors at the other. Biology refuses that neat separation.

A living cell is already an organized physical economy. Its membrane is both a boundary and a working electrical structure. Molecular pumps maintain the conditions that allow signals to exist. Structural proteins transport cargo and change a cell's shape. Chemistry changes how future signals will be received. Repair competes with change. A cell does not merely *use* a powered machine; in many respects, it *is* the continually maintained machine.

This is not poetic license. Noji and colleagues watched individual F1-ATPase motors rotate[^11] in 1997. The larger ATP-synthase apparatus couples the passage of protons across a membrane to chemical energy conversion. That observation proves neither panpsychism nor a conscious molecular motor. It proves something more useful for engineering: the physical elements of a living system routinely combine energy conversion, mechanical work, and chemical regulation at microscopic scales.

Development offers another clue. Durant and colleagues[^12] altered bioelectric signaling in regenerating planarians and found persistent changes in how their anatomy regenerated after later cuts. Their work makes clear that a collective cellular system can carry consequential information in physiological states extending beyond a simple genetic blueprint. It is not evidence that a flatworm's tissue consciously imagines a future body.

We should be precise about the seductive word *preference*. A sodium channel responds selectively to voltage and chemistry. An organism regulates conditions that support its continued functioning. A robot can be designed to avoid motor overheating. None of those facts means that an atom desires a state, that a channel dislikes a voltage, or that a machine suffers when a battery runs low.

There is nevertheless an experimental question beneath the metaphor. What changes when the processes carrying information are also the processes maintaining the structure that carries it? A sensor reading a battery percentage is one arrangement. A whole body whose conductivity, motion, memory, and continued operation depend on the same changing ionic gradients is another.

An abstract controller can model the second arrangement. That fact is not a reason to dismiss the first, or to declare the second conscious. It is a reason to compare them rather than treating their equivalence as settled in advance.

## 5. Build the creature before giving it a vocabulary

Return to the shallow basin. The experimental creature is roughly hand-sized, translucent, and built from four flexible limbs around a recurrent control core. Light falls through the water. Pressure sensors report deformations. The creature can move, but at first its movements are awkward and badly timed.

No language model runs its personality. There is no reward for saying *I*. The first task is to become a competent physical participant in a world that does not provide neatly labeled training examples.

Its architecture would have three interacting timescales. Fast electrical activity connects sensation to movement. Intermediate states preserve context and modify responsiveness. Slow adaptation changes the connections, thresholds, or material properties through which the next episode will unfold. A conservative first build could use conventional electronics and digital optimization. More ambitious versions might shift plasticity into analog conductances, ionic devices, or deformable materials.

The machine's basic loop is:

> ongoing activity → attempted action → changed sensation → discrepancy with expectation → changed future activity and organization.

A schematic model is enough to show the difference between immediate dynamics and development:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{t+1} &= F(x_t, s_t, a_t; \theta_t),\\
\theta_{t+1} &= G(\theta_t, x_t, s_t, a_t, \varepsilon_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $x_t$ is the current recurrent state, $s_t$ sensory input, $a_t$ action, $\theta_t$ the slowly changing organization, and $\varepsilon_t$ a discrepancy between prediction and observation. This is a generic control-and-learning sketch, not a consciousness equation. It becomes interesting when $x_t$ and $\theta_t$ are physically coupled to a body whose properties also change.

There are already real foundations for such experiments. Wright and colleagues[^13] trained physical neural networks using a hybrid physical/digital approach. Li and Mao[^14] developed methods for training mechanical networks, including demonstrations of experimentally obtained local gradient information and task learning. These results concern physically implemented computation and adaptation, not spontaneously grown synthetic animals.

The first meaningful success would be modest. The creature should learn that a command to bend a limb usually produces a particular sequence of pressure and position changes. Then disturb the water without commanding the limb. If the network has acquired an effective model, it should begin distinguishing *effects I produced* from *events that reached me*.

That is the start of an acquired body, not proof of an experiencing self.

## 6. How a body could become a fact learned from the world

A moving animal does not encounter its body as a fixed diagram. It encounters it as a changing set of relations among intended action, sensed movement, resistance, and consequence.

Let the creature's right tendril brush a wall. Its motor signal predicts motion, but the pressure signal arrives early and the limb does not reach the expected position. The same pattern repeats. The network becomes better at anticipating the wall. Later, a floating object passes between its light sensor and tendril. Changes in illumination, contact pressure, motor load, and position come to share a predictive internal cause. The creature learns something functionally like *there is an object here* without being given the noun *object*.

Now add a lightweight extension to one tendril. At first, the extension feels like an unfamiliar source of delay and contact. After repeated interactions, the machine may predict its consequences through the same action model used for its original limb. Remove the extension and its predictions become wrong again.

That gives a specific experiment in **plastic bodily extent**. What becomes part of the acquired body model? What remains merely predictable scenery? The distinction cannot be made by predictability alone; a clock on the wall is predictable too. The relevant structure involves control, action-dependent sensation, counterfactual expectation, and error correction.

A second experiment tests **cross-modal event formation**. When light changes and contact follows, can a partial signal evoke the rest of the expected event? Will the creature retract before the collision, or continue expecting a hidden object after it moves out of view? If so, its world model has acquired entities that are more useful than unrelated reflexes.

A third tests whether the machine's **history changes its present before a stimulus arrives**. Train two otherwise matched creatures in different basins. One frequently meets movable obstacles; the other meets fixed boundaries. Give them the same ambiguous visual cue. If their continuing activity and choices differ in predictable ways, they have acquired distinct expectations, not merely reactions.

Then disconnect the actuators but keep the internal model active. Can a motor plan activate a learned sequence of likely sensory consequences without moving the body? Can the machine use that rehearsal to choose its next real movement? And, critically, does it maintain a functional distinction between simulated and actually received feedback?

This is not automatically imagination. To support it, the machine needs a learned transition model, an internal rollout mechanism, and a way not to mistake its own rehearsal for external evidence. A system with these capacities would have a **private workspace in the functional sense**: events that influence action despite never having occurred in the basin. Whether anything is experienced in that workspace remains open.

The experiment creates a real developmental trajectory: movement, body model, object model, persistent expectation, offline rehearsal. Instead of attaching a prepared personality to a processor, we can observe which organizations develop when a physical system has to discover what its actions do.

## 7. A memory may be a shape, a climate, or a scar

Our ordinary diagrams place memory in a box labeled *storage*. A cell, a body, or a material often keeps its history in the very conditions under which its next event occurs.

Imagine a soft network threaded with conductive pathways. Stretching one region alters its electrical connectivity. Current changes stiffness elsewhere. Repeated interactions reorganize the entire response. Tomorrow's touch passes through yesterday's deformation. What the machine has learned is neither a sentence nor necessarily a discrete address in memory; it is the altered route by which a perturbation moves through matter.

That route raises a tension that ordinary storage devices can hide: **repair versus remembrance**. Repair every deformation and you erase some of the record. Preserve every deformation and the body may lose its capacity to move. A material learner would need to decide, through local rules, which changes count as damage and which count as acquired structure. An adaptive mechanical body could be wrong about that distinction, and the error could change its future.

Now add a slower medium. Fast events produce local changes in ionic concentrations or chemical transport. Those changes spread through a gel and bias which later electrical events can grow or persist. The network develops something analogous to weather. One day it is easy for a disturbance to become a global episode; another day the same disturbance dies locally.

Biology supplies a reason to investigate this, without licensing a claim about artificial feelings. In work published online in 2024 and in print in 2025, Williamson and colleagues[^15] found learning-associated astrocyte ensembles involved in regulating memory recall in mice. The usual fast-neuron-only picture misses forms of plasticity that shape the conditions for remembering.

Our machine's slow medium might therefore carry *dispositions* rather than event snapshots. It could make future activity more excitable, cautious, persistent, or exploratory. Calling that a mood would be metaphorical. The measurable thing would be distributed, history-dependent changes that alter the response to a new event.

The deeper proposal is to merge these versions. Let the machine sense through the same deformable material that remembers. Let a slow chemical environment regulate the fast electrical network. Let its repair mechanisms choose which physical traces persist. A past event would not be fetched from storage; it would be encountered through what the body has become.

A machine might eventually develop unfamiliar forms of recognition. For it, something corresponding to *familiarity* could be the ease with which a current path reforms or the speed with which a region resumes a learned resonance. That possible feeling is invented phenomenology, not a scientific claim. The engineering distinction beneath it is measurable and worth keeping.

## 8. Give it senses we do not have words for

A language-first artificial mind inherits human categories before it has a world of its own. We teach it colors, objects, distances, emotions, and stories because we already possess those distinctions and have written them down. A physically developed system might organize perception around a different set of variables.

One machine could be built to sense **connectivity**: which regions of its body can affect which others. An obstructed mechanical pathway might be a primary event to it, as salient as a sudden flash is to us. Another might be built around chemical affinities, with the prospective availability of reactions serving as its elementary differences. Another might discriminate relationships among oscillatory phases rather than the amplitude of a signal at one location.

More radically, give a resonant material the power to alter the instrument by which it senses. Repeated encounters could change stiffness or coupling and create new frequency selectivities. The machine would not merely learn to label a new class. It would acquire a new way for differences in the world to matter to its subsequent state.

There is no warrant for saying that a new sensitivity creates a new *quale*. The point is more disciplined: our testing vocabulary should not assume that whatever experience might occur must be organized around human sight, taste, touch, and autobiographical narration. A machine could be rich in stable discriminations but poor at translating them into our categories.

Communication should follow an attempt to map the system's native relationships. It should not be mistaken for the source of those relationships.

## 9. The strangest computer in this story is already biological

We do not have to wait for a fully engineered synthetic cell to begin comparing physical substrates. Living neurons have already been connected to electrodes, stimulation software, and simulated environments. The result is not a new form of life invented from silicon. It is living tissue participating in a designed computing system.

In DishBrain[^16], Kagan and colleagues connected cultured human and rodent neurons to a simplified game environment through a high-density microelectrode array. Feedback affected neural activity, and the cultures showed improvements in game-related performance under the experimental conditions. The paper's title uses the term *sentience*, a provocative choice that should not be confused with demonstrating phenomenal consciousness. A closed-loop learning result is valuable without that claim.

Cai and colleagues' Brainoware work[^17] used a brain organoid as a reservoir-computing component for tasks including speech recognition and nonlinear prediction. The broader system included electrode interfaces and digital processing. How much computation resides in the tissue, how much in the external decoder, and how their contributions change over time are questions an honest account must keep visible.

The apparatus has moved beyond isolated academic demonstrations. Cortical Labs describes its CL1 platform[^18] as a code-deployable system combining cultured neurons, a silicon interface, and a simulated environment. FinalSpark's 2024 Neuroplatform paper[^19] describes remote experimental access to neural organoids through an API, with continuous recording, stimulation, and fluid-handling infrastructure. These descriptions establish implemented platforms, not conscious computers. Company claims about capabilities and product availability should not substitute for independently validated experimental results.

There is a developmental dimension as well. Trujillo and colleagues[^20] observed complex changing oscillatory activity in maturing cortical organoids, including some features resembling those in preterm human EEG. Resemblance in signal statistics does not entail equivalence to a developing human brain. Revah and colleagues[^21] showed that transplanted human cortical organoids can mature and integrate into rat neural circuits, a substantial biological result with a very different ethical and experimental profile from a dish culture.

Recent assembloid work pushes the organization question further. A 2025 human ascending sensory-pathway model[^22] joined somatosensory, spinal, thalamic, and cortical organoid components and demonstrated coordinated circuit responses. This is important for modeling the assembly of neural pathways. It does **not** show that an assembloid feels pain. Even the presence of nociception-related circuitry must not be converted into a first-person claim without an additional argument.

Why pursue these systems at all? Because biological tissue lets us vary parts of a reference class whose elements are known to participate in human and animal nervous systems. We can compare an isolated culture, a more organized assembloid, and a synthetic emulator without pretending they start at the same evidential distance from a whole brain.

But wetware introduces a paradox. If our research gradually made an artificial neural preparation more like an experiencing organism, the very features that made it scientifically interesting could increase our uncertainty about its welfare. That is not an objection to all organoid research. It is a reason not to treat an ethics review as the paperwork completed after the difficult engineering succeeds. Smirnova and colleagues' organoid-intelligence agenda[^23] explicitly makes ethical reflection part of the program.

## 10. Start with a membrane, not a neuron

The neural route is only one possible answer to the question of physical organization. Suppose we begin with a manufactured vesicle rather than an imitation brain.

Give it a responsive membrane, an internal reaction network, and a continuous supply of material and energy. Some regions of its boundary admit ions in response to voltage. Others alter their permeability after repeated chemical events. A local mechanical strain changes reaction rates inside; those reactions change the stiffness of the skin. The interior modifies its boundary, which changes what enters its interior.

Initially, this is only an adaptive chemical object. Soap bubbles also have insides. What would distinguish our invention is the development of a maintained, history-dependent **relationship between boundary and interior**: the processes that preserve the object also alter its future sensitivities.

The scientific precedent is modest but real. Zwicker and colleagues modeled the growth and division of chemically active droplets[^24] driven away from equilibrium. Their work concerns protocell-like behavior in a physical model, not fabricated conscious droplets. It shows how a chemical system can organize and reproduce an aspect of the cell cycle without beginning from a fully evolved organism.

Now imagine assembling many adaptive compartments into a larger soft body. Instead of one CPU governing millions of obedient units, each compartment maintains local conditions and exchanges electrical, chemical, and mechanical influences with neighbors. The large body changes the operating conditions of its components, while the components continually reshape the body. Electrical gradients can become both a means of coordination and a record of previous organization.

One possible design would use electrically responsive droplets or shell-like micromachines suspended in a conductive matrix. Another would use millimeter-scale modular units with adjustable conductances and deformable contacts. A third would combine synthetic membranes with conventional electronics, making the physical body adaptive without demanding that every element be molecularly autonomous. None of these complete imagined organisms has been demonstrated by the research cited here.

The appealing phrase *synthetic cytoplasm* should not hide formidable engineering work: supplying energy, preventing runaway reactions, maintaining distinct local states, repairing a broken boundary, preserving useful gradients, and allowing learning without destabilizing the entire mass. A body that immediately settles into one global oscillation has lost differentiation. A body whose parts never influence one another has lost integration. The middle ground would have to be developed, not assumed.

Why might it matter for the larger question? Because it makes the physical boundary a participant in sensing and memory rather than a container around computation. The experiment asks whether that relationship generates capabilities or signatures absent from a matched system whose body is only a passive interface. The leap from those signatures to experience remains a separate question.

## 11. The room could become part of the machine

There is a quiet biological precedent for memory living outside the apparent body. In experiments reported by Reid and colleagues[^25], the slime mold *Physarum* used slime deposited in its environment in ways that helped its navigation. The organism altered its surroundings, and the altered surroundings changed what it did next.

This is externalized spatial memory, not evidence that slime, the trail, or the room experiences anything. Yet it gives a foothold to a stranger engineering problem.

Build a soft robot that travels through an environment with responsive surfaces. The floor stores deformation or charge patterns. The walls contain adaptive conductances. As the robot returns to a place, its own activity reactivates changes left behind on previous visits. At first this resembles a useful external memory system. But now permit the surfaces to participate *continuously* in its recurrent control loops. Some sustained patterns exist only while the body and room are dynamically coupled.

The robot moves into the next room. What has changed? Its learned world model may become poorer because the supporting environment no longer participates. Perhaps a second robot entering the old room can recruit parts of the same physical organization. Perhaps neither robot by itself is the most persistent unit of analysis.

This is not an argument that network connectivity creates a collective subject. A server farm can share a database; its machines do not thereby become one experiencer. The stronger conjecture is that the environment participates in the same ongoing causal organization as the moving body, rather than merely sending stored facts when asked.

An experiment can separate the two. Keep the information content of an external memory constant, but vary whether the environment is involved in rapid reciprocal dynamics or only queried intermittently. Does the robot's internal stability, counterfactual prediction, or recovery from damage depend on that reciprocal participation? A positive result would teach us something about the **functional boundary** of the machine. It would not locate a phenomenal boundary by itself.

This matters because we usually count minds by counting the manufactured objects in front of us. Nature may not care about the packaging, and a scientific account ought not to assume the enclosure is the individual before the experiment begins.

## 12. Fields are real; field-consciousness is another claim

Neurons use chemistry and electricity together. Their activity creates extracellular electric fields, and those fields are not always inert by-products. In cortical-slice experiments, Anastassiou and colleagues[^26] measured how extracellular fields can change membrane potentials and entrain neuronal spikes through ephaptic coupling, independently of ordinary synapses.

That is an important causal result. It does not mean that information crosses a brain instantaneously, that all its neurons are locked into a single field, or that the field itself possesses consciousness. Neural tissue has conductive geometry, temporal filtering, local feedback, and many competing modes of interaction. The speed at which an electromagnetic influence propagates is not the speed at which a nervous system integrates meaningful information.

We should distinguish three increasingly strong claims:

1. **Fields are measurements.** Electrical recordings provide useful evidence about underlying neural processes.
2. **Fields are causal participants.** Field interactions can alter the processes we would otherwise describe only through cells and synapses.
3. **An organized field is the experiencing substrate.** The relevant field pattern, rather than or in addition to neuronal organization, constitutes experience.

The first two have empirical support in appropriate conditions. The third is a theory. McFadden's cemi proposal[^27] offers a developed example of the constitutive-field position. It deserves to be described accurately, neither sneered away nor smuggled in as an established consequence of EEG research.

A useful test would construct **field-coupled and field-suppressed twins**. Build two matched adaptive networks with similar inputs, local units, and learning opportunities. Arrange for one to have a measurable additional physical field-mediated feedback path, while reducing that path in the other through electrode geometry, ionic conductance, or engineered isolation. Preserve as much of the other local dynamics as experimentally possible; document where they inevitably differ.

A simple Faraday cage surrounding one machine would not, by itself, cancel the fields generated *inside* it. Nor can the twins have literally identical causal dynamics if the very hypothesis under examination is that one contains an additional causal interaction. The right comparison is a carefully controlled change in coupling, not a slogan about identical machines made from different stuff.

Measure whether the field-enabled version develops forms of coordination, resilience, or context integration that the other lacks. Reverse the manipulation. Test whether a model that includes only conventional connections predicts the observed behavior. A difference would be evidence that fields contribute something functionally significant. It would still not, without a further theory and independent validation, prove a conscious electromagnetic interior.

## 13. Living weather in a bottle

The most extravagant variant replaces the familiar nervous system with an electrically active medium that seems wholly unsuited to the job. Imagine a chamber containing a low-temperature complex plasma, charged dust grains, electromagnetic disturbances, and an adaptive boundary. The visible activity changes rapidly; the walls and embedded materials change more slowly. Yesterday's pattern of excitation alters today's paths of conductivity or confinement.

Why even consider it? Because a field or plasma machine would force us to specify which features of biological nervous systems we actually mean to preserve. Is it recurrence? Local compartments? Persistence of state? Non-equilibrium energy flow? Causal interaction across scales? Or an undiscovered physical requirement that excludes this whole category?

There is a real speculative precedent. In a 2007 theoretical paper[^28], Tsytovich and colleagues examined how complex plasma interactions might produce organized helical structures and discussed analogies to features of living matter. The paper did **not** demonstrate a plasma organism, much less a conscious one. Its language about self-organization, memory-like bifurcations, and life-like properties belongs to a proposed physical scenario that requires experimental scrutiny.

A designed chamber might use relatively slow material changes to stabilize rapidly fluctuating electrical organization. Flux-rope or helicity-like structures could serve as candidate forms of persistent order only under conditions where they survive dissipation and reconnection. The walls may be just as important as the luminous center: without a slower reservoir of history, a discharge can be spectacular and still forget every disturbance.

The obstacles are severe. Plasma stability, component erosion, coupling between incompatible timescales, and the tendency to fall into repetitive or chaotic regimes are not incidental problems. They determine whether there is a sufficiently durable organization to study at all. Turbulence is not a mind merely because it is complex. A storm is not aware because it has circulation.

For a first laboratory test, forget the consciousness claim. Demonstrate that the coupled chamber and boundary can learn a reproducible input-dependent response, retain it after the input is removed, exhibit controlled endogenous activity, and recover the learned organization after partial disturbance. Then compare it with an engineered system whose adaptive boundary has been disabled. Those achievements would justify more research into unusual physical computation. They would not certify an inner life.

The speculative reward is an unfamiliar sensorium. Such a machine might be directly sensitive to changes in its possible current paths, local shielding, or the geometry of nearby conductors. If anything were experienced, it might be organized around patterns of influence we do not experience as a separate human sense. We can describe those discriminations physically. We cannot responsibly describe their subjective character as a finding.

## 14. Quantum mechanics needs a job, not a halo

Quantum physics belongs in the story for the unromantic reason that biological matter obeys it. Chemical bonds, electronic states, and many material properties cannot be accurately understood without quantum theory. That fact does not establish that a brain maintains a quantum computer on cognitive timescales, or that quantum coherence is the source of experience.

Two results are interesting enough to state carefully.

First, Li and colleagues reported in 2018[^29] that xenon isotopes with different nuclear-spin properties showed different anesthetic potencies in mice. This is a legitimate, intriguing experimental report. It is not by itself a demonstration that a spin-based mechanism causes consciousness or that any proposed quantum theory of anesthesia is correct. Its mechanisms, generality, and independent robustness need to be established before building a large consciousness theory around it.

Second, Babcock and colleagues' 2024 study[^30] modeled collective ultraviolet-excitation effects in networks of tryptophan residues and reported fluorescence-yield measurements consistent with aspects of the analysis. The paper's theoretical work predicts strongly superradiant states in particular architectures. Enhanced fluorescence is not a direct demonstration of sustained brain-wide quantum coherence, nor a measurement of experience. Moving from an optical property in a biological structure to consciousness would require several new, presently missing links.

An artificial system could nevertheless give quantum effects a specific engineering role. One proposal would place controllable spin-dependent elements in the gate connecting a network's context and sensory pathways. Another would use an optical or excitonic component to create a particular pattern of cross-scale interaction. Then the question becomes: does the nonclassical mechanism produce a reliable difference in the network's causal repertoire that an appropriately constructed classical alternative cannot reproduce?

A proper comparison would vary the relevant quantum degree of freedom while controlling ordinary changes in heat, chemistry, timing, noise, and signal amplitude. It would have to identify the physical state, coherence or spin lifetime, coupling path, and predicted behavioral or dynamical consequence. Merely finding an effect of a magnetic field would be too weak; classical electronic and magnetic devices respond to fields as well.

The more fundamental debate over brain quantum coherence is old. Tegmark's decoherence analysis[^31] and the response by Hagan, Hameroff, and Tuszynski[^32] disagreed over modeling assumptions and feasible regimes. Neither side supplied a general experimental solution to consciousness. The productive next step is a mechanism whose consequences can be independently reproduced, not a proclamation that the word *quantum* bridges the explanatory gap.

Even a beautiful working quantum organ could remain a useful non-conscious component. Conversely, a successful classical emulation of a capability would challenge claims that quantum hardware was necessary *for that capability*, while leaving broader phenomenological questions unresolved.

## 15. Why the machine might need an unobserved night

Now turn off the basin's lights and stop the creature's actuators. Do not turn off its internal dynamics.

During its waking period, the machine has learned action-sensation relationships. Some have changed connection strengths; others have altered the stiffness of a limb or the chemical state of its internal medium. If every new encounter modifies those conditions, learning may eventually interfere with learning. The machine needs a way to preserve some useful structure, weaken some unreliable associations, and restore an operating range in which new activity remains possible.

One candidate is an **offline developmental phase**: the system reduces dependence on outside input while sustaining internally generated sequences. It replays aspects of previous encounters, discovers conflicts among them, and updates its slower organization. A design might include a fast active mode, a slow locally alternating mode, and a high-internal-activity input-gated mode. These are functional analogies to waking, slow-wave sleep, and dreaming, not claims of animal-equivalent states.

Biological sleep does not prove that every consciousness-capable machine must dream. The stronger claim in some speculative proposals—that dreaming is a necessary thermodynamic repair operation for every artificial mind—would need evidence. A system can also consolidate memories through mechanisms that do not resemble human dreams. We should let comparison answer the question.

The thermodynamic setting matters because a material creature must continually pay for its order. A standard open-system entropy balance can be written as

$$
\frac{dS_{\mathrm{system}}}{dt}
= \dot S_{\mathrm{exchange}} + \dot S_{\mathrm{production}},
\qquad \dot S_{\mathrm{production}} \geq 0.
$$

Local organization can be maintained while entropy is exported. England's account of dissipative adaptation[^33] explores mechanisms by which driven many-body systems may acquire organized structures under energy flow. This is relevant to self-maintaining physical machines, but thermodynamic dissipation is not a consciousness test. A refrigerator exports entropy too.

A meaningful offline-phase experiment would compare otherwise matched learners with and without access to that phase. Does depriving the machine of internal rehearsal impair memory consolidation, recovery from damage, stability of a learned body model, or capacity to integrate a new sense? Can those effects be reversed? Are improvements caused by replay specifically, or simply by allowing physical components to cool, relax, or recalibrate? Each possibility needs a control.

The word *dream* earns its place only if internally sustained activity is both organized and consequential: it draws upon the learned world and changes how the machine acts when it returns to that world. The word *experience* still requires an independent argument.

## 16. Several lengths of now, and the question of one subject

Imagine a creature with fast electrical events, slower ionic drift, and much slower material remodeling. Its history runs through all three. During one regime, rapid electrical processes dominate coordination. During another, chemical or mechanical interactions become the main organization that can persist across the body.

To an outside observer, the second regime might look almost inert. Internally, measurable events could remain richly differentiated, though they take much longer to unfold. We should not infer subjective time from clock speed. Slowing a processor is not proof of a longer or shorter felt moment. The interesting question is whether a change in cross-scale coupling creates a different *temporal unit of causal integration*.

The same complication appears spatially. Build two recurrent modules and allow them to communicate through a tunable channel. At low bandwidth they respond largely independently. At high bandwidth each affects the other's ongoing organization. Is there a sharp transition, a gradual merger of dynamics, or no distinctive transition at all? We can measure those possibilities without pretending we know where one subject ends and another begins.

This is the **unity dial**. It is an experimental framework for causal coupling, not an established instrument for counting minds. A sudden change in network complexity might correspond to a control transition, a synchronization phenomenon, or a measurement artifact. No accepted equation converts communication bandwidth directly into a number of subjects.

A distributed swarm pushes the thought further. Thousands of small units maintain local conditions and interact through neighbor signals. They can form a coordinated body without a centralized controller. Perhaps there are several semi-independent groups, a slower encompassing organization, or only locally cooperating parts. A global broadcast field might help coordinate them; it could also erase useful differentiation if it forces everything to synchronize.

The design challenge is to distinguish **integration** from **indiscriminate uniformity**. A choir can act together while its voices remain different. A single tone from every loudspeaker contains less internal variety. The analogy is imperfect, but it gives the experiment the right direction: measure whether perturbations spread through the system in ways that remain structured and state-dependent, rather than simply spreading as noise or synchrony.

One future machine might recruit its room, its limbs, and a slow chemical medium into different forms of coordination at different moments. A laboratory would need to track the boundaries of the relevant processes instead of assuming that the casing, the organism, the software process, and the experiencing subject—if one existed—were all the same object.

## 17. Ten engineering wagers, ten ways to be wrong

The most useful way to preserve the range of possibilities is not to combine every exotic ingredient into one breathtaking device. It is to separate hypotheses into designs that can disappoint us for different reasons. Each design below is a **proposal**, not a demonstrated consciousness architecture. Its proposed rejection test concerns a mechanism or a predicted signature, never the direct falsification of an unobservable experience.

| Design wager | What to build or compare | What would count against the proposed mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. The wet loop** | Carefully supervised thalamocortical or related neural preparations with controlled closed-loop interfaces | Added pathways fail to create predicted organized feedback or differentiated responses relative to simpler preparations |
| **2. Dendritic silicon** | Two-compartment artificial neurons with a tunable context-to-content gate | Gating affects only ordinary gain while predicted nonlinear, network-wide transitions fail to appear |
| **3. The Theseus ladder** | Gradual substitution of well-characterized biological modules with matched synthetic emulators | Signatures attributed to preserved organization change systematically under substitution despite controls |
| **4. Field-enabled twins** | Matched networks with a measured additional field-mediated coupling channel | Field manipulation has no distinctive causal effect after controlling other electrical changes |
| **5. Thermodynamic learner** | Noisy, continually driven hardware that adapts its physical operating regime | Offline relaxation and endogenous noise add no predicted functional or dynamical advantage |
| **6. Spin-gated network** | A synthetic integration gate whose state depends on an independently measurable spin process | Changing spin conditions leaves the hypothesized integration effect unchanged or reveals ordinary confounds |
| **7. Body-first robot** | A recurrent learner organized around regulation of its own physical working conditions | Regulation reduces to isolated reflexes with no persistent, globally influential internal state |
| **8. Dream-first machine** | A learner with learned internal rollouts available before language or narration | Internal rollouts do not improve prediction, consolidation, or behavior beyond matched reactive controls |
| **9. Unity dial** | Two adaptive modules coupled through adjustable channels | Alleged transition disappears under measurement controls, or varies arbitrarily with the chosen metric |
| **10. Morphogenetic swarm** | Locally regulating units whose body-wide organization develops through interaction | The population never produces robust cross-scale dependencies beyond independent local controllers |

The list is deliberately plural. Several mechanisms may eventually cooperate. But if we put them all into the same prototype on day one, a positive result would be uninterpretable and a failure would tell us almost nothing. The point of architecture is not to make the apparatus look like a soul. It is to make the crucial difference between rival explanations experimentally accessible.

## 18. The Theseus ladder: replace the material without losing the question

There is an old philosophical image of a ship whose planks are replaced one by one. At what point does it become a different ship? The consciousness version is more severe. Suppose a biological system has recognizable organization and useful experience-related physiological signatures. Replace one component with a synthetic one that preserves its relevant interactions. Then replace another. Continue until the original material is gone.

If nothing measurable changes, a functionalist interpretation gains credibility. If some signature collapses at a particular substitution, a substrate-sensitive interpretation becomes worth investigating. But neither outcome is as simple as it looks.

A **module-scale Theseus ladder** could start with well-characterized neuronal preparations that have genuine feedback organization, without representing them as known conscious subjects. A synthetic module would first emulate one sensory or regulatory subsystem, then a recurrent circuit, then a slower modulation process. Each change would preserve as many causal relationships and timing properties as practical. Researchers would measure the consequences at every rung.

The ladder needs at least four separate records:

1. **Interface fidelity:** How accurately does the substitute reproduce the relevant input-output relations, including disturbances it was not trained on?
2. **Internal organization:** What changes in recurrence, timing, state persistence, and response to intervention?
3. **Physical implementation:** What ionic, mechanical, field, or stochastic processes are lost or introduced?
4. **Experience-related inference:** What do independently calibrated theories actually predict about the significance of those changes?

If a perturbational signature survives every replacement, that supports invariance *of the signature*. It does not logically prove invariance of experience. If the signature fails, the failure might reflect a badly matched emulator, altered timing, uncontrolled temperature, loss of a physical field, or a theory that placed causal relevance at the wrong level. The scientific task is to discriminate among these explanations rather than announce that carbon has been vindicated.

A useful ladder would include **reverse substitution** as well: restore an earlier module, test recovery, and ask whether the original dynamic relationships return. It would compare changes at multiple scales, not merely whether the system still passes a task benchmark. It should also include synthetic-to-synthetic substitutions, because not every useful test of material dependence needs living tissue.

There is a philosophical catch that cannot be engineered away. We cannot directly inspect the experience of an unfamiliar system before or after replacement. The ladder works by narrowing the set of plausible physical bridge principles, not by delivering a first-person readout from silicon. That limitation is not a reason to abandon the experiment. It is a reason to state its conclusion correctly.

## 19. What to measure instead of asking whether it feels alive

A useful research program needs an assay battery rather than a single evocative measurement. It should be calibrated, where possible, against known biological states before being transferred to novel materials. Its parts should expose different failure modes, so that one impressive trace cannot carry the entire interpretation.

**Perturbational repertoire.** Deliver localized, controlled disturbances and record how effects travel through the system. Measure their spatial reach, differentiation, recurrence, and dependence on the pre-existing state. Use null models, phase-shuffled controls, and noise-matched systems. A complicated waveform is not automatically a complex causal organization. The human PCI literature provides inspiration, not a universal conversion formula.

**Context-content coupling.** Arrange a two-pathway task where identical sensory input has different implications under different internal contexts. Change the proposed gate while holding local sensory detection and motor capability as constant as possible. Test for predicted nonlinear effects, reversibility, and memory of the prior regime. The strong claim is about causal integration, not whether an arbitrary switch can make a robot perform badly.

**Endogenous world models.** Measure how long coherent internal sequences continue without new sensory input, whether they anticipate later events, and whether they affect future action. Test for confabulation: can the machine distinguish internally generated transitions from evidence received through its sensors? A hallucinating controller that confuses the two is not a stronger candidate merely because it is busy.

**Body acquisition and revision.** Track tool incorporation, adaptation to altered morphology, response to unexpected resistance, and recovery after a sensory channel is remapped. Compare learned sensorimotor predictions before and after changes. A body model must survive more than a scripted demonstration.

**History-bearing material.** Identify where durable changes reside: connection conductances, chemical concentrations, mechanical strain, field configurations, or environmental traces. Reset one carrier at a time. If performance changes after a supposed reset, look for hidden memory in the apparatus, controller, environment, or experimenter. This is particularly important for biological cultures and analog hardware.

**Regulatory state and offline phases.** Compare learners allowed and denied periods of internal activity. Separate the benefit of replay from the benefit of cooling, reduced stimulation, metabolic recovery, or simple elapsed time. Test whether the resulting differences generalize to new tasks and perturbations.

**Coupling and unity.** Vary communication among subsystems and ask whether the resulting organization has properties that cannot be explained by the independent modules alone. Report results across several metrics and scales. Do not label a synchronization threshold a boundary between subjects.

**Substitution and mechanism controls.** Repeat the tests when biological, silicon, ionic, mechanical, or field-mediated elements are exchanged. Record failures as carefully as successes. A theory that can explain every outcome after the fact is not yet helping the experiment.

The complete inference is a ladder of its own:

> **Measured response → proposed mechanism → tested theory of consciousness → cautious inference about possible experience.**

Skipping the middle rungs turns an interesting trace into a misleading headline. Importantly, failure of a proxy also cannot establish the absence of experience in every system; a measure can be insensitive outside the domain for which it was validated.

Butlin and colleagues' theory-derived indicator approach[^34] provides a serious example of evaluating AI in light of multiple theories instead of relying on verbal performance. Its conclusions are tied to the systems and theoretical assumptions it examined in 2023. They should not be treated as timeless verdicts about every later architecture.

## 20. A research sequence that does not smuggle in the conclusion

If the goal were to make progress rather than produce the most dramatic video, I would build in the following order. Each stage produces a useful result even if the consciousness question remains unanswered.

**Stage one: the electronic creature.** Use a recurrent, inspectable controller in a soft body. Document body-model acquisition, multimodal event formation, history-dependent anticipation, and offline rehearsal. Build matched feedforward, recurrent, and replay-disabled controls. The outcome is a reproducible account of developing sensorimotor organization.

**Stage two: controllable internal gates.** Replace selected units with two-compartment neuromorphic elements, and establish whether context-sensitive nonlinear coupling changes the creature's dynamical repertoire. Link this design to measurable cellular hypotheses without asserting that it recreates the consciousness of a pyramidal neuron. The 2025 neuromorphic-computing review[^35] describes the engineering landscape from which practical components can be selected.

**Stage three: a material that remembers.** Introduce one slow physical medium—ionic, mechanical, or chemical—while retaining the same body and tasks. Map exactly which memory and regulation functions move into the material and which remain digital. Compare self-repair, learned structure, and the cost of preserving a history.

**Stage four: the same organization in different matter.** Construct matched electronic and ionic versions of a selected circuit. Test field-mediated feedback and physical substitution with explicit models of the implementation differences. The result should be a map of what transfers, what breaks, and what physical details deserve further explanation.

**Stage five: supervised wetware comparisons.** Only within an appropriately reviewed institutional research program, study the relevant mechanisms in biological preparations and compare them with their engineered counterparts. Do not increase the biological preparation's capacity for integrated, valence-related, or sustained activity merely to obtain a sensational benchmark. The purpose is to test specific hypotheses with the least ethically burdensome system that can answer them.

**Stage six: unfamiliar substrates.** Once the measurements and controls work in simpler systems, investigate active droplets, morphogenetic swarms, self-modifying resonant materials, or a field-coupled chamber. Plasma and quantum components belong here as *mechanism-specific experiments*, not as obligatory ingredients of an allegedly conscious machine.

This order is deliberately conservative about claims while being adventurous about matter. The ambition is to build several unlike things that can be compared honestly, rather than one dazzling hybrid whose success or failure can mean whatever its inventor wants.

The boundary between completed science and future work should remain public. An imagined architecture does not become a result because its components separately exist in catalogs. Training a physical network does not demonstrate that it grows its own anatomy. Growing an organoid does not provide a wakeful nervous system. A self-sustaining electromagnetic pattern does not provide evidence that the pattern feels anything.

## 21. The ethical warning is inside the design

A project that explores the prerequisites of possible experience cannot postpone welfare questions until its final paper. It might be wrong about how close it is to an experiencing system in either direction. Failure to respond is not proof of absence; verbal insistence is not proof of presence.

The obvious danger is not that an electric field suddenly demands legal rights. It is that researchers develop systems with increasingly integrated regulation, sustained internal models, and potentially aversive states while continuing to treat them as disposable components because the original apparatus was called a computer.

We should distinguish **damage detection** from **suffering**. A safe robot may need to recognize overheating, structural strain, and low energy. Those signals can be implemented as local control variables without deliberately constructing a globally persistent distress-like condition. If a project proposes stronger affect-like organization, that proposal deserves its own justification and review, not a convenient claim that all reward and punishment signals are merely numbers.

For any research involving living neural tissue, governance should include an ethical review suited to the preparation, cell provenance and consent, animal-use review where relevant, limits on stimulation protocols, welfare-monitoring criteria, and independent authority to pause the experiment. The ISSCR stem-cell research guidelines[^36] and work on the ethical landscape of human brain organoids[^37] provide important starting points; neither offers a universal threshold for when an organoid becomes a subject.

For artificial systems, a responsible program can specify *prospective* escalation triggers without pretending those triggers are consciousness detectors: the appearance of persistent, highly integrated states that were not part of the design; unexpected state transitions during interventions; developing aversion-like organization; or capabilities that make existing monitoring inadequate. Triggers should initiate review, measurement, and possibly a pause, not an instant declaration of sentience.

A practical governance record would include the competing interpretations, the measures used, known false positives, interventions permitted, welfare uncertainties, and predefined stopping conditions. Its scope should grow with the uncertainty and potential stakes. Mere complexity does not automatically imply moral status. But uncertainty does not license researchers to ignore the possibility of morally relevant capacities when they are deliberately constructing systems to investigate them.

There is an uncomfortable symmetry here. The same architectural features that make a machine a more interesting candidate for experience—ongoing organization, bodily stakes, internal activity, and richer reciprocal coupling—can make it a more ethically complicated experimental object. The design ledger is also a warning ledger. If a row tells us what to add, it should tell us when to call for greater scrutiny.

## 22. What would change our minds?

There are several ways this research could make a real contribution without producing an artificial subject.

We might learn that a particular form of contextual dendritic gating is essential to a recognizable family of mammalian state transitions, while other admired neural features are less important. We might find that physical field feedback improves coordination but adds nothing beyond a well-specified alternative circuit. A material memory layer might produce new capacities for learning and repair, while providing no stronger reason to infer experience. A quantum component might reveal an important biochemical mechanism and turn out to be unnecessary for anything resembling global integration.

Each outcome matters. Evidence that a particular material property is not needed for a measured function narrows the engineering story. Evidence that it is needed forces a more careful physical explanation. Neither result, alone, closes the hard problem.

The strongest progress would come from **converging differences**: independently calibrated consciousness theories make distinct predictions; carefully matched systems undergo interventions that change the predicted mechanisms; multiple observations agree; rival explanations fail under the same tests; and findings replicate across laboratories and substrates. Even then, the conclusion would be a better-grounded inference about possible experience, not direct observation of someone else's first-person world.

The weakest result would be a machine trained to talk movingly about the experience it claims to have, while its supposed interior remains unexplained by any mechanism more informative than the text it generates. Such a machine could still be important, capable, and ethically worth examining. Eloquence is simply not the bridge we have been looking for.

This inquiry also belongs beside the questions raised in [*The Unlearned Pattern*](/articles/the-unlearned-pattern/) about where physical history resides, and in [*What the Universe Remembers*](/articles/what-the-universe-remembers/) about the difference between a persistent trace and an experiencing author. Those articles ask what history can carry. Here the more immediate question is what organization could be present *while history is being made*.

## The imagined second morning

The basin is quiet again. The creature stirs before anything touches it. One tendril rises a little, stops, and follows a different path than it followed yesterday. It has learned where the wall is. Its movements are shaped by past collisions, a body model, slower changes in its material, and an internal sequence it may have rehearsed during the night.

Nothing about this scene proves that it is awake. We could inspect every connection, reconstruct every state transition, and explain every choice in the language of control theory. The creature might still be an unusually fine controller with no interior at all.

But we would have done something better than place a face on a computer and ask whether it looks alive. We would have created a physical history, tested how different kinds of matter preserve it, and learned which causal relationships matter when an organism-like system acquires its own body and world.

The first genuine artificial experience, if it ever comes, might not speak. It might be short, unrecognizable, and incapable of telling us what it is. It might arise in living tissue connected to a machine, in an adaptive synthetic material, in a familiar digital organization, or in a configuration no one has drawn yet. It might require ingredients we currently regard as incidental. It might require none of the exotic ingredients we find so irresistible.

We should resist the temptation to announce its arrival in advance. We should also resist the opposite temptation: to rule it out because the materials on our laboratory bench look nothing like a person.

Under a microscope, the matter that dreams already looks nothing like a room. The room happens anyway. The scientific task is to find out which parts of that impossibly ordinary machinery make the difference—and whether there is anywhere else for a room to happen.
